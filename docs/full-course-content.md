# Chapter 2 — Choose How You'll Sell

## The question before the question

Most people start by asking "what should I sell?" That is actually the second question. The first question is: how will you sell it?

The answer shapes everything that follows. It affects how much money you need to start, how quickly you can have something for sale, and how much of the process you control. The exciting part is that some of these models let you start selling within days, with very little money upfront.

There are five common models. Each one works. The right choice depends on where you are right now, and you can always move to a different model later as your skills and confidence grow.

[IMAGE: A horizontal spectrum graphic showing the five sourcing models from left (quickest start, lowest cost) to right (more investment, more control). Each model is represented by a simple icon. Left to right: shopping bag (reselling), paintbrush on t-shirt (print-on-demand), arrows between two points (dropshipping), tag/label icon (private label), pencil and blueprint (design and manufacture). Below the spectrum: "Start here if you want to learn fast" on the left, "Grow into this as you gain experience" on the right. Friendly, approachable style.]

## The five models

### 1. Reselling — the fastest way to start

You buy products that already exist (wholesale, clearance, or through suppliers) and sell them at a higher price. This is the simplest model and you can genuinely start within days.

The beauty of reselling is that the learning curve is short. You find a good product, list it, and focus entirely on selling. This teaches you the skills that matter most early on: how to write a listing, how to price competitively, and how to handle customers.

Your [TOOLTIP: margins | The amount of money you keep after subtracting all your costs from the selling price. If you buy something for £8 and sell it for £15, your margin is £7.] are typically thinner than other models, but that is a fair trade for speed and simplicity. Many successful store owners started here and moved to other models once they knew what sold well.

### 2. Print-on-demand — sell your designs, hold no stock

You create a design and a partner company prints it onto products (t-shirts, mugs, phone cases, posters) and ships it directly to the customer. You only pay when someone orders, so there is zero upfront product cost.

This is a brilliant way to test creative ideas and build an audience. If you have a talent for design or an eye for what a specific community would love to wear or display, this model lets you turn that into a product with almost no financial risk.

Margins are tighter than some other models because the print company takes their share, but you are trading margin for freedom: no stock to buy, no products to ship, and no leftover inventory if something does not sell.

### 3. Dropshipping — test demand without buying stock

You list a product in your store and when someone buys, a supplier ships it directly to them. You never handle the product yourself.

This can work well as a demand testing tool. You can quickly find out whether anyone wants a product before committing to buying stock. The important thing is to go in with realistic expectations: shipping times can be longer (especially with overseas suppliers), and you are relying on someone else for quality and delivery speed. Choose your suppliers carefully and start with one or two products rather than a large catalogue.

Many sellers use dropshipping to identify winning products, then switch to holding stock or private labelling once they have evidence that something sells well.

### 4. Private label — your brand, built on proven demand

You find an existing product from a manufacturer, add your own branding and packaging, and sell it as your own. This gives you stronger margins and a product that feels genuinely yours.

This model takes longer to set up because you will need to order samples, agree on quantities with the manufacturer, and wait for production and shipping. A realistic timeline from first enquiry to products in hand is around 6 to 12 weeks.

[IMAGE: A friendly timeline illustration showing the private label process as a simple journey with milestones: Find supplier → Request samples (1-3 weeks) → Review and approve → Place first order (2-4 weeks production) → Shipping (2-6 weeks) → Ready to sell. Use a road or path metaphor rather than a clinical process diagram. Each milestone has a small encouraging icon. The overall feeling should be "this is a journey with clear steps" not "this is a long and complicated process."]

The key with private labelling is that it works best when you already know what sells. That is why many founders start with reselling or dropshipping, validate demand, and then move to private label for the products that proved themselves. You are not guessing what to manufacture. You are upgrading something you have already sold.

Platforms like [TOOLTIP: Alibaba | A wholesale marketplace where manufacturers (mostly in China) sell products in bulk. You can find suppliers for almost any type of physical product and many will add your branding to their existing products.] make finding manufacturers accessible. We will cover how to communicate with suppliers and evaluate them in Chapter 5.

### 5. Design and manufacture — build something truly unique

You create a completely new product from scratch and have it manufactured. This is the path with the highest potential because you own something nobody else can sell.

For most people starting out, this is the model to grow into rather than start with. Learn how to find customers, test demand, and run a profitable store first. Once those skills are in place and revenue is coming in, designing your own product becomes a much more confident move because you already know what your customers want.

## You are not locking yourself in

This is important: your choice here is a starting point, not a permanent decision. The selling skills you build (finding customers, writing great listings, running ads, reading your numbers) transfer across every model.

Many of the most successful online sellers started with the simplest model available to them and upgraded over time. Start where you can start now. You will know when you are ready for the next level because the course will help you recognise that moment.

[LOOP: Not sure which model to choose? Pick reselling or print-on-demand. Both let you start quickly with minimal cost, and everything you learn applies to any model you move to later. You can always come back to this chapter and make a different choice.]

## Before you move on

Fill in the worksheet for this chapter. You are choosing a sourcing model and writing down why it fits your current situation. There are no wrong answers here. "Because I can start quickly and learn as I go" is a perfectly good reason. You will have plenty of time to get more ambitious once you have made your first sale.

Speaking of which, the next step is finding something to sell. And that is more fun than you might expect.

# Chapter 3 — Brainstorm With Discipline

**Estimated reading time:** 10 minutes
**Worksheet:** Product Ideas worksheet
**Canvas connection:** Product Candidates section populates

---

## This is where good ideas come from

Not from staring at a blank page. Not from copying what someone else is selling. And not just from thinking about what you personally would buy.

Good product ideas come from evidence. Real people, spending real money, on real products, right now. Your job in this chapter is to find that evidence. And the great news is that it is hiding in plain sight, in places you probably already visit.

By the end of this chapter you will have a shortlist of product ideas backed by actual demand signals. That is a much better starting position than most new sellers ever have.

## Marketplace bestseller lists: your first goldmine

Amazon, eBay, and Etsy all show you what is selling well right now. This is not secret information. It is publicly available, free, and incredibly useful.

On Amazon, browse the "Best Sellers" section within categories that interest you. On eBay, search for products and filter by "completed listings" to see what actually sold (not just what was listed). On Etsy, search a category and sort by number of reviews or relevance.

What you are looking for: products that sell consistently across multiple sellers. This tells you the demand is real and not dependent on one specific brand or listing. Consistent demand across many sellers is one of the strongest signals you can find.

## Search patterns: is interest growing or fading?

[TOOLTIP: Google Trends | A free tool from Google that shows how often people search for a particular term over time. It tells you whether interest in something is growing, stable, or declining. You can compare multiple search terms side by side.] is free and takes 30 seconds to check any product idea. Type in what you are considering and look at the search interest over the last 12 months.

A stable or gently rising line means consistent demand. That is ideal for a first product because you will have steady opportunities to learn and improve.

A sharp spike followed by a drop means the trend has peaked. You might still catch the tail end, but you are more likely to be building a store around yesterday's demand.

[IMAGE: Two simple Google Trends style line charts side by side. Left chart labelled "Steady demand — great for a first product" shows a gently fluctuating but stable line across 12 months with a subtle upward drift. Right chart labelled "Trend spike — the opportunity may have passed" shows a sharp peak followed by a decline. Both charts should feel clean and easy to read, not technical. Below: "Spend 30 seconds checking this for every idea. It could save you months."]

## Review mining: find gaps other sellers are missing

This is one of the most valuable research methods available and it is completely free.

Go to Amazon or any marketplace and read the 1-star, 2-star, and 3-star reviews of products in a category you are considering. Ignore the 5-star reviews for now. Focus on what people are unhappy about.

"Broke after two weeks." "Sizing was completely wrong." "Looks nothing like the photo." "Great product but the instructions were useless."

Every complaint is a potential opportunity. If a popular product consistently gets the same criticism, there is room for someone who solves that problem. You do not need to invent a new product. You might just need to offer a better version of something people already buy.

## Social listening: hear what people are asking for

Go where your potential customers talk. Reddit, Facebook groups, hobbyist forums, YouTube comment sections. Search for phrases like:

- "does anyone know where to find..."
- "I wish someone would make..."
- "looking for recommendations..."
- "I have tried everything and..."

These are real people describing unmet needs in their own words. Pay attention to the language they use. It tells you not only what to sell but how to describe it in a way that resonates.

## Check for seasonality

[TOOLTIP: Seasonal demand | When a product sells significantly more during certain times of the year. Christmas decorations, swimwear, and back-to-school supplies are obvious examples, but many products have subtler seasonal patterns. A product with seasonal demand can absolutely be profitable, but your timing and planning need to account for the quieter months.] can catch you out if you are not aware of it. A product might look like it has no demand when really you are just looking at the wrong time of year.

Check Google Trends for a full 12-month view of any product idea. If you see a big spike at one time of year and a flat line otherwise, factor that into your planning. Year-round demand is usually the better choice for a first product because it gives you consistent opportunities to test and improve.

## Competition: is there space for you?

Finding demand is exciting. But before you get too attached to an idea, take a quick look at who else is selling it.

Search for your product on the marketplaces where you would list it. Look at the top results. How many reviews do they have? How good are their photos and descriptions? Are they all large established brands, or is there a mix?

If the top sellers have thousands of reviews and polished professional listings, standing out will take more time and budget. That does not mean it is impossible, but it is worth knowing.

The sweet spot for a new seller is strong demand with imperfect supply. Products where existing sellers have mediocre listings, inconsistent quality, slow shipping, or obvious gaps in how they serve the customer. That is your opening.

[IMAGE: A simple, friendly 2x2 matrix. X-axis: "Lower demand" to "Higher demand". Y-axis: "Weaker competition" to "Stronger competition". The four quadrants are labelled with short, encouraging descriptions: Top-left "Niche — small but can work nicely", Top-right "Sweet spot — this is where you want to be", Bottom-left "Skip this one", Bottom-right "Tough — possible but you will need to work harder for it". The top-right quadrant is highlighted with a subtle glow or colour. The overall style should feel like a helpful map, not an academic chart.]

## Boring can be profitable (and that is a good thing)

Here is a secret that experienced sellers know: some of the most reliable products to sell are the ones nobody brags about.

Replacement parts for common appliances. Organisational supplies for specific hobbies. Niche templates and tools for small businesses. Storage solutions for particular situations.

These products share three powerful characteristics: the demand is steady (people need them regardless of trends), the buyer knows exactly what they want (so they search for it and are ready to buy), and the purchase often repeats.

A customer who buys a replacement filter buys another one in six months. A customer who buys a storage organiser that fits their specific hobby recommends it to friends who share that hobby.

Do not filter ideas by how exciting they sound at a dinner party. Filter them by how strong the evidence is. A "boring" product with proven, repeating demand is a far better foundation for a business than a trendy product with a short shelf life.

## Score and shortlist your ideas

By now you should have several possibilities. The worksheet for this chapter asks you to capture your top three to five ideas along with the evidence you found for each one.

For each idea, note:
- What demand evidence you found and where you found it
- What the competition looks like and where the gaps are
- Whether demand is year-round, seasonal, or trend-dependent
- How well it fits the sourcing model you chose in Chapter 2

You are not making a final decision yet. You are building a shortlist of ideas that have earned further investigation. The next chapter helps you set the rules that will keep your testing disciplined. And the chapter after that puts these ideas through the numbers to see which ones can actually make you money.

[LOOP: If none of your ideas show strong demand signals, that is genuinely useful information. It means you have avoided investing time and money into something that would not have worked. Head back to the research methods, explore different categories, and try again. Many successful sellers tested several ideas before finding the one that clicked. The research skills you are building here will make your next round faster and sharper.]

## You are making real progress

Think about where you were at the start of this chapter. You had a blank page. Now you have a shortlist of product ideas backed by real evidence: marketplace data, search trends, customer complaints, and competitive gaps.

Most people who try to sell online never do this research. They pick something based on a hunch and hope for the best. You are already ahead of them.

Next up: setting the rules that will keep you focused and protect your time and money as you move forward. This is where your business gets its backbone.

# Chapter 4 — Set Your Founder Rules

**Estimated reading time:** 10 minutes
**Worksheet:** Founder Rules Sheet
**Canvas connection:** Operating Constraints, Decision Framework, and Boundaries sections populate. Cross-check: does minimum startup cost from Chapter 2 fit within money cap?

## Your ideas deserve protection

You finished the last chapter with a shortlist of product ideas backed by real evidence. That is exciting. And excitement is exactly why you need this chapter.

Excitement makes you spend too fast, work without structure, and hold on to ideas long after the evidence says to move on. The founders who succeed are not the ones with the most passion. They are the ones who channel their passion through a set of simple operating rules.

Your Founder Rules are not about making this feel rigid. They are about making decisions easier. When you know how much time you are committing, how much money you are willing to spend, and what results would tell you to stop, continue, or invest more, you do not waste energy second-guessing yourself every week.

Think of these rules as a gift to your future self. You are making the hard decisions now, when you are thinking clearly, so that future-you does not have to make them under pressure.

## Time rules: small and steady wins

You do not need 40 hours a week to build a business. You need a few focused hours, consistently, week after week. The key word is consistently.

Four focused hours every week for 12 weeks will get you further than a single 14-hour marathon followed by three weeks of doing nothing. Your brain needs repetition to build momentum. Your business needs regular attention to move forward.

Here is what to decide now:

**How many hours per week can you realistically give this?** Not the ambitious number. The honest number. The number you could sustain even during a busy week when life gets in the way. For most people starting out, that is somewhere between 5 and 15 hours.

**When exactly will you work on this?** Pick specific days and times. "Tuesday and Thursday evenings 7 to 9" is a rule. "Whenever I get a chance" is a wish. Protect your chosen time blocks the way you would protect a work meeting or a doctor's appointment.

**When will you review your progress?** Set a weekly review slot. This is 20 to 30 minutes where you look at what happened, what the numbers say, and what you will focus on next week. Sunday evening works well for many people. The day does not matter. Having it in the calendar does.

[IMAGE: A simple weekly calendar view (Monday to Sunday) with a few time blocks highlighted in a friendly accent colour, showing example work sessions on Tuesday evening, Thursday evening, and Saturday morning. A small "review" icon on Sunday evening. The visual should feel like a calm, achievable schedule, not a packed corporate calendar. Plenty of white space to show that most of the week is still free.]

## Money rules: spend on evidence, not hope

One of the most common early mistakes is spending money on things that feel productive but do not actually test anything. A premium logo, a custom website theme, three months of software subscriptions before your first listing is live. That is spending on hope.

The alternative is spending on evidence. A small ad budget to test whether anyone clicks. A few product samples to check quality. A marketplace listing fee to see if anyone buys.

Here is what to decide:

**What is your monthly maximum?** This is the total amount you can spend each month without it causing stress. Be honest. If £200 is your comfortable limit, write down £200. You can increase it later when revenue starts coming in.

**How much goes to tools and how much goes to testing?** Tools are the recurring costs that keep your business running: your store platform, email software, design tools. Testing is the money you spend learning whether something works: ad spend, product samples, marketplace fees. Early on, most of your budget should go to testing because that is where the learning happens.

A simple starting split: keep your tool costs as low as possible (many platforms have free tiers or free trials) and direct the rest toward testing real ideas with real customers.

## Decision rules: know when to stop, continue, or push harder

This is the most valuable part of your Founder Rules and the part most people skip.

Before you start testing any product idea, you need to decide in advance what the results would need to look like for you to:

**Stop** and try a different idea. Not because you failed, but because the evidence told you this one is not the right fit right now. This is discipline, not defeat.

**Continue** and give it more time. Because the early signs are encouraging even if the results are not dramatic yet.

**Invest more** because the results are strong enough to justify increasing your time, budget, or effort.

Write these in your own words and make them specific to your situation. At this stage you probably will not use technical metrics because you have not run any tests yet. That is perfectly fine. Write what makes sense to you now:

- "I will stop if nobody shows interest after three weeks of active testing."
- "I will continue if I get questions and engagement but sales are slow."
- "I will invest more if I make consistent sales within my first month of listing."

These rules will get sharper as you gain experience. In later chapters, when you are running ads and reading real data, you will come back and update them with specific numbers. For now, the important thing is having them written down at all.

[IMAGE: A simple visual showing three paths branching from a central decision point. Left path labelled "Stop — try a different idea" with a small redirect arrow icon. Middle path labelled "Continue — give it more time" with a small clock icon. Right path labelled "Invest more — the numbers support it" with a small growth icon. The visual should feel like a clear, calm decision tree, not a stressful crossroads. Each path should feel like a positive outcome because each one is a clear, informed decision.]

## Data over ego

Here is a commitment worth making now, before you need it:

Your personal attachment to an idea is not a reason to keep going. Evidence is.

You can genuinely like a product category and still walk away from a specific product within it if the numbers do not support it. You can feel unsure about a product and still pursue it if the evidence is strong.

This is not about ignoring your instincts. It is about not letting your instincts overrule what the data is telling you. The best founders are the ones who can say "I loved this idea but it did not work, and that is okay because now I know."

Write a version of this commitment in your own words. It can be the sentence above or something that resonates more personally. The act of writing it down makes it real.

## Red-line rules: your non-negotiables

These are the boundaries you will not cross regardless of how things are going. They protect you from the most common traps that pull new sellers off track.

Here are some examples. Pick the ones that feel right and add your own:

- "I will not skip my weekly review."
- "I will not add a new tool or subscription without removing one I am not using."
- "I will not increase my spending beyond my monthly cap without hitting my escalation criteria."
- "I will track every expense and every sale from day one."

That last one matters more than it might seem right now. Keeping a record of your income and expenses from the very start (even a simple spreadsheet) saves enormous hassle later. Depending on where you live, you may also need to look into registering as self-employed or understanding basic tax requirements for selling online. A quick search for "selling online tax rules" plus your country will point you in the right direction. This is not complicated but it is worth doing early rather than scrambling to sort it out months down the line.

## You now have an operating system

Look at what you have built in this chapter. A realistic time commitment. A spending limit that protects your finances. Decision rules that will tell you what to do when results come in. A commitment to following the evidence. And a set of non-negotiable boundaries.

This is your operating system. It is the foundation that makes everything else in this course work. When you feel unsure about a decision in a later chapter, come back here. Your rules will tell you what to do.

Next up: putting your product ideas through the numbers to see which ones can actually make you money. This is where the shortlist gets shorter, and that is a very good thing.

# Chapter 5 — Know Your Numbers Before You Commit

**Estimated reading time:** 12 minutes
**Worksheet:** Unit Economics worksheet (per candidate idea)
**Canvas connection:** Product Economics section populates for chosen idea. Cross-checks: does stock investment fit within money cap (Ch4)? Does variant complexity match budget?

---

## The chapter that saves you the most money

This is not a finance lesson. This is a survival skill.

For every product you sell, there is a simple question: after all the costs are paid, how much do you actually keep? If the answer is "not much" or "less than nothing," it does not matter how many people want the product. You will lose money selling it.

This chapter teaches you how to work that out before you spend anything. By the end, you will know exactly which of your shortlisted ideas can make money and which ones cannot. Some ideas will not survive this chapter, and that is the whole point. Better to find out now with a calculator than later with an empty bank account.

## The maths is simple (genuinely)

[TOOLTIP: Unit economics | A fancy term for a simple idea: how much money you make (or lose) on each single product you sell. If you sell a phone case for £15 and it costs you £9 in total to source it, ship it, and process the payment, your unit economics are positive — you make £6 per sale. If those costs add up to £16, your unit economics are negative and you lose money on every sale.]

Here is the formula:

**Selling price minus all costs equals your margin.**

That is it. The skill is in making sure you have counted all the costs, because the ones you miss are the ones that quietly eat your profit.

## The costs most beginners miss

When you think about product cost, you probably think about what you pay the supplier. That is only the starting point. Here is the full picture:

**Product cost.** What you pay per unit to buy or manufacture the product.

**Shipping to you** (if you are holding stock). The cost of getting products from the supplier to your home or storage space. For overseas suppliers this includes freight and potentially customs duties.

**Shipping to the customer.** What it costs to send each order. This is a big one. If you offer "free shipping," you are paying for it out of your margin. If you charge the customer for shipping, your product appears more expensive than competitors who include it in their price.

**Packaging.** Boxes, mailers, tape, tissue paper, thank-you cards. Individually small costs that add up across hundreds of orders.

**Platform fees.** Most selling platforms take a percentage of each sale. This is typically between 3% and 15% depending on the platform.

**Payment processing.** Stripe, PayPal, or your platform's built-in payments will take roughly 2% to 3% of each transaction.

**Returns.** A percentage of orders will come back. For most product categories, plan for 3% to 10% of orders being returned. Each return costs you the original shipping, the return shipping (if you cover it), and the product may not be resellable.

Here is a worked example:

| Cost line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Selling price | £25.00 |
| Product cost | -£7.00 |
| Shipping to customer | -£3.50 |
| Packaging | -£1.00 |
| Platform fee (10%) | -£2.50 |
| Payment processing (3%) | -£0.75 |
| Returns allowance (5%) | -£1.25 |
| **Margin per sale** | **£10.00** |

In this example, you keep £10 out of every £25 sale. That needs to cover your advertising costs (which you will learn about in Chapter 12) and your tool subscriptions, and still leave you with profit.

If that margin were £2 instead of £10, you would need to sell five times as many products to make the same money, and a small increase in any cost could tip you into losing money on every sale.

[IMAGE: A simple, visual "waterfall" chart showing money flowing from the selling price at the top down through each cost layer, arriving at the margin at the bottom. Each cost layer is a different soft colour. The margin at the bottom is highlighted in a positive green. The visual should make the concept feel clear and approachable, like watching water flow through levels, not like reading an accounting spreadsheet.]

## Shipping: the cost that shapes your pricing

Shipping deserves special attention because it affects both your costs and how your customer perceives your price.

You have three options:

**Free shipping (you absorb the cost).** Customers love this. But you are paying for it, so your product price needs to be high enough to cover it. A £15 product with "free" £4 shipping means you are really selling at £11 margin before other costs.

**Charged shipping (customer pays).** Your product price looks lower, but the total at checkout is the same. Some customers abandon their cart when they see shipping added. Others expect it and do not mind.

**Flat-rate shipping.** A middle ground. You charge a fixed shipping fee regardless of order size. Simple for the customer to understand and simple for you to manage. You might lose a little on heavy orders and gain a little on light ones, but it averages out.

There is no universally right answer. What matters is that you know your shipping cost per order and factor it into your unit economics before you set your price. Check your local postal service rates (Royal Mail, USPS, or equivalent) for the package sizes and weights your products would require.

## Product complexity: when one product becomes twenty

Some products are straightforward. A phone case, a kitchen tool, a digital template. One [TOOLTIP: SKU | Stock Keeping Unit. Each unique version of a product gets its own SKU. A t-shirt in 3 sizes and 4 colours has 12 SKUs. A one-size phone case in 1 colour has 1 SKU. More SKUs means more stock to buy, more listings to manage, and more complexity overall.] to manage, one listing to write, one set of stock to buy.

Other products multiply quickly. Clothing is the classic example. A single t-shirt design in 5 sizes and 4 colours is 20 SKUs. Each one needs stock. If your supplier has a [TOOLTIP: minimum order quantity (MOQ) | The smallest number of units a manufacturer will produce per SKU. If the MOQ is 50 per size per colour, a 20-SKU t-shirt range requires ordering 1,000 units minimum.] of 50 per variant, you are buying 1,000 units before you have sold a single one.

Jewellery has similar complexity. Different materials, sizes, lengths, and finishes each create a new variant that needs stock and its own listing details.

This does not mean you should avoid these categories. It means you need to know what you are signing up for. If your monthly budget from Chapter 4 is £300, a 20-SKU clothing range is not your first move. But a single design in two sizes and one colour might be very achievable.

**The principle:** start with the minimum viable range. The smallest number of variants that lets you test demand credibly. If it sells, expand. If it does not, you have risked much less.

## Talking to suppliers

If your sourcing model from Chapter 2 involves suppliers (wholesale, private label, or manufacturing), you need real numbers from real suppliers, not estimates from your imagination.

This can feel intimidating if you have never done it before. It is actually straightforward. Suppliers want to sell to you. That is their business. You are not bothering them by asking questions.

**Where to find suppliers.** Alibaba for manufacturing and private label (mostly China-based). Wholesale directories and trade shows for domestic suppliers. A simple Google search for "[your product] wholesale supplier [your country]" will surface options.

**What to say.** Keep your first message short and professional. Here is a template that works:

> "Hello, I am interested in [product name]. Could you please provide: price per unit for quantities of [50 / 100 / 500], minimum order quantity, sample cost and shipping time, and production lead time for a first order. Thank you."

That is all you need. You do not need to pretend you are a large company. Many suppliers work happily with small businesses and new sellers.

**What to look for in responses.** Quick replies are a good sign. Detailed answers to your questions are a good sign. Willingness to send samples is a good sign. Vague answers, pressure to place a large order immediately, or inability to provide samples are warning signs. If a supplier will not let you check the product before committing, find a different supplier.

**Samples are essential.** Never place a bulk order without seeing and testing a sample first. Yes, samples cost money (usually the product cost plus shipping). This is one of the best small investments you can make. A £20 sample that reveals poor quality saves you from a £500 order of products you cannot sell.

## Score your ideas with real numbers

Now it is time to put each of your shortlisted ideas through the numbers.

For each idea from Chapter 3, fill in the unit economics in your worksheet. Use real numbers wherever possible: actual supplier quotes, actual shipping rates, actual platform fees. Where you do not have real numbers yet (such as advertising costs), use conservative estimates. It is always better to discover your margins are healthier than expected than to find out they are thinner.

Once you have run the numbers, you will likely find that some ideas have strong margins and some do not. The ones with thin or negative margins get eliminated here, regardless of how promising the demand signals were.

This is your data-over-ego commitment from Chapter 4 in action. A product with fantastic demand and terrible margins is not a business. It is a way to stay very busy while losing money.

[LOOP: If none of your ideas survive the numbers, take a breath. This is the process working exactly as it should. You have just avoided investing weeks of effort and real money into products that would not have been profitable. Head back to Chapter 3, explore different categories or price points, and run the numbers again. Each round gets faster because you now know exactly what to look for. Many successful sellers went through two or three rounds of ideas before finding the one that worked.]

## You now know which idea can actually make money

Not which idea sounds best. Not which idea you like most. Which idea has real demand (Chapter 3) and real margins (this chapter).

If you have one or more ideas that passed both filters, you are in a strong position. The next chapter is where it gets real: you are going to test your idea by listing it for sale on an existing marketplace and finding out whether someone will actually hand over money for it.

# Chapter 6 — Test Before You Build a Store

**Estimated reading time:** 10 minutes
**Worksheet:** Pre-Store Test worksheet
**Canvas connection:** Pre-Store Validation section populates. Cross-check: test result triggers decision framework from Chapter 4.

---

## Your first sale could happen this week

You have a product idea backed by evidence. You know the numbers work. Now the question is: will someone actually buy it?

You could spend the next few weeks building a full online store to find out. Or you could list it on a marketplace where millions of buyers are already shopping and get your answer much faster.

That is what this chapter is about. Testing demand with real customers, in a real marketplace, before you invest in building your own store. If it sells, you have powerful validation that justifies everything that comes next. If it does not, you find out cheaply and quickly.

## Why marketplaces first

Marketplaces like eBay, Vinted, Etsy, Amazon, and Facebook Marketplace already have the one thing a new store does not: buyers. Millions of people visit these sites every day specifically looking to purchase things.

When you list on a marketplace, you are borrowing their audience to test your product. You do not need to drive traffic, build trust from scratch, or set up payment processing. The infrastructure is already there.

If your product does not sell when it is placed in front of an existing audience of active buyers, that is a very clear signal. It means the issue is likely the product, the price, or the listing itself, not a lack of traffic. That clarity is incredibly valuable because it tells you exactly what to investigate next.

If your product does sell, you have something even more valuable: proof that a stranger was willing to pay real money for what you are offering. That is stronger evidence than any survey, focus group, or gut feeling.

## Choose your test marketplace

Different marketplaces suit different product types. Pick the one that best matches what you are selling:

**eBay** works well for almost anything, especially resold products, electronics accessories, home goods, and collectibles. It has a large, diverse buyer base and it is easy to set up a listing.

**Vinted** is ideal if you are testing fashion, clothing, or accessories. The audience is specifically looking for these categories.

**Etsy** suits handmade items, craft supplies, digital products (templates, printables, planners), and vintage goods. The buyers on Etsy expect unique or creative products.

**Amazon** suits products with high search volume where buyers are comparing options. Setting up as a seller takes slightly more effort, but the audience is enormous.

**Facebook Marketplace** is useful for local testing. If your product works well for a local audience (bulky items, locally relevant products), this lets you test without shipping.

You only need one marketplace for this test. Pick the one where your target buyer is most likely to be shopping and move on.

## Write your first listing

You will learn how to write polished, high-converting listings in Chapter 9. For now, you just need something clear, honest, and good enough.

**Title:** What it is, who it is for, and one key detail. "Waterproof Dog Seat Cover, Universal Fit, Heavy-Duty Material" tells the buyer everything they need to know. Save the clever branding for later.

**Description:** What the product does, what is included, the key measurements or specifications, and shipping information. Answer the questions a buyer would have before they have to ask them.

**Price:** Use the unit economics from Chapter 5. Make sure your price covers all costs and leaves you with the margin you calculated. Check what similar products are selling for on the same marketplace. You do not need to be the cheapest, but you need to be in a range the buyer considers reasonable.

**Photos:** Take clear, well-lit photos. Natural light near a window is better than a flash. A clean, uncluttered background. Show the product from multiple angles. If it is a physical product, include something for scale (a hand, a common object) so the buyer knows what they are getting. You do not need professional photography at this stage. You need clarity and honesty.

[IMAGE: A before-and-after comparison of product photos. Left side "Before" shows a product photographed on a cluttered surface with poor lighting and a harsh flash shadow. Right side "After" shows the same product on a clean white surface near a window with soft natural light, taken from a slight angle. The difference should be obvious and the "After" should look achievable, not studio-professional. Caption: "Five minutes of setup makes this difference."]

## Set your test duration

Decide in advance how long you will run this test before judging the results. This connects directly to your Founder Rules from Chapter 4.

A reasonable test duration for a marketplace listing is 2 to 4 weeks. Less than two weeks often is not enough time to draw conclusions, especially on platforms where your listing needs time to gain visibility.

Write down your test duration before you list the product. This prevents you from pulling the listing after three days because you are impatient, or leaving it up for three months because you are avoiding making a decision.

## When it sells: ship it well

This might be the first time you ship a product to a customer. It is simpler than you think, and it is also more important than you might expect.

**Pack it properly.** The product should arrive in the same condition it left. Use appropriate packaging for the product type. Fragile items need padding. Soft items need a waterproof mailer. Over-packaging slightly is better than under-packaging.

**Ship it promptly.** Aim to dispatch within one to two business days of the order. Quick dispatch builds trust and leads to better reviews. Most marketplace platforms show the buyer when the item was shipped, so delays are visible.

**Add tracking.** Use a tracked shipping service whenever the cost is reasonable. It protects both you and the buyer. If a package goes missing and there is no tracking, you will usually need to refund the customer.

**Communicate.** A brief message to the buyer confirming dispatch and providing the tracking number goes a long way. It shows you are attentive and professional. Many sellers skip this. Standing out is easy when the bar is low.

## Read the results

After your test period, look at what happened:

**It sold.** Excellent. How quickly? How many units? Did the buyer leave positive feedback? Did anyone ask questions that suggest they were interested but needed more information? Every data point here is useful.

**Interest but no sale.** People viewed the listing, asked questions, or added it to their watchlist, but nobody bought. This suggests the demand is there but something about the listing needs work. Common causes: price too high relative to competition, photos not compelling enough, description missing key details, or the listing title is not surfacing in search results.

**Views but no engagement.** People saw the listing but did not click, ask questions, or purchase. This might mean the product is not compelling enough at a glance, or the title and main image are not grabbing attention. Or it might mean you need more time or a different marketplace.

**Very few views.** The listing is not being seen. This is usually a search visibility issue. Your title might not contain the words buyers are searching for. Try updating the title with terms from your demand research in Chapter 3.

## Make your decision

This is where your kill/continue/escalate rules from Chapter 4 come in. You wrote them specifically for this moment.

Based on the evidence from your test:

**Proceed to building your store** if the product sold or generated strong interest. You have validated demand with real customers in a real marketplace. That is a powerful foundation for everything that comes next.

**Iterate and retest** if the results were mixed. Adjust the price, rewrite the listing, take better photos, or try a different marketplace. Give it another test cycle before deciding.

**Pivot to a different product** if there was little to no interest despite a fair test. This is not failure. This is exactly what the test was designed to discover. Return to your shortlist from Chapter 3 and Chapter 5. Pick the next strongest candidate and test again. You will be faster this time because you now know the process.

[LOOP: If your first product test did not work out, remember where you are. You have researched demand signals, run unit economics, tested on a real marketplace, and learned from the results. That puts you ahead of the vast majority of people who try to sell online. Pick your next idea, run the test again. The loop gets faster every time because your skills improve with each round.]

## What this test proves (and what comes next)

A successful marketplace test proves that someone who does not know you is willing to pay real money for your product. That is powerful validation.

What it does not prove is that you can build a profitable, repeatable business around it. That depends on whether you can drive your own traffic, acquire customers at a sustainable cost, and encourage repeat purchases. Those are the skills you will build in the chapters ahead.

But you now have something most aspiring sellers never get: evidence. Not a guess, not a hope, not a plan. A real transaction with a real customer.

If your test went well, the next phase is exciting. You are going to build your offer, set up your store, and start driving your own customers to it.

You have earned the momentum. Let's keep going.

# Chapter 7 — Pick Your Customer

**Estimated reading time:** 10 minutes
**Worksheet:** Customer Profile worksheet

## You already know more than you think

You have tested your product on a real marketplace. You have seen who viewed it, who asked questions, and who bought. That is real customer data, even if it is a small sample.

This chapter takes what you have learned so far and sharpens it into a clear picture of who your customer actually is. Not a vague idea of "people who might be interested." A specific person you can find, speak to, and serve better than anyone else.

Getting this right makes everything that follows easier. Your product listing practically writes itself when you know exactly who you are talking to. Your marketing becomes simpler because you know where they spend time. And your offer becomes stronger because you know what they actually care about.

## Why "everyone" is not a customer

It feels logical to want to sell to as many people as possible. In practice, trying to sell to everyone means you end up speaking to no one in particular.

Think about it from the buyer's perspective. If you are a first-time dog owner living in a small flat, looking for a compact, easy-to-clean seat cover for your car, which listing would you click?

"Car seat cover. Fits all vehicles. Great for pets, kids, and general use."

Or:

"Compact dog seat cover designed for small cars. Waterproof, folds flat for easy storage, machine washable."

The second listing describes fewer people. But the people it does describe feel like it was made for them. They are far more likely to click, read the full listing, and buy.

This is the power of being specific. You are not excluding customers. You are attracting the right ones.

[IMAGE: Two simple side-by-side product listing previews. Left listing has a generic title "Car Seat Cover — Universal" with a bland stock-style photo and a generic description. Right listing has a specific title "Compact Dog Seat Cover for Small Cars" with a more targeted photo (showing a dog on a seat cover in a smaller car) and a description that speaks to a specific buyer. The right listing has a subtle highlight or glow suggesting it is the stronger option. Both should look like realistic marketplace listing thumbnails.]

## Define your niche customer

A [TOOLTIP: niche | A specific, well-defined segment of a larger market. Instead of "pet owners" (huge market), a niche might be "first-time dog owners in apartments" or "cat owners who travel frequently." Niches are easier and cheaper to reach because you know exactly where to find them and what to say.] customer is not a tiny market. It is a focused starting point. You start specific, build a reputation with that group, and expand later from a position of strength.

Here is how to define yours. Answer these questions as specifically as you can:

**Who feels this problem most?** Not everyone who could use your product, but the person for whom it solves a genuine, recurring frustration. The person who has already tried other solutions and been disappointed. The person who would be relieved to find something that actually works.

**What is their situation?** Think about their life, not just their purchase. Are they short on time? On a budget? New to something and feeling overwhelmed? Managing a specific challenge? The more you understand about what surrounds the problem, the better you can speak to it.

**What words do they use?** This is crucial. Your customer probably does not describe their problem using the same words you would. Go back to the reviews and forum posts from Chapter 3. Look at how people actually talk about the problem. Those phrases belong in your listing and your marketing because they are the words your customer will type into a search bar.

Here is an example of a generic customer description versus a useful one:

**Generic:** "People who like dogs and have cars."

**Useful:** "First-time dog owners, aged 25 to 40, who drive smaller cars (hatchbacks, city SUVs) and are looking for a seat cover that does not take up the whole back seat. They want something that is easy to clean because they are still figuring out the messy reality of dog ownership. They are likely searching on Google or Amazon with phrases like 'small car dog seat cover' or 'easy clean dog seat protector.'"

The second version tells you what to sell, how to describe it, where to advertise, and which search terms to target. That is a customer profile that actually works.

## Find where they gather

Your customer is already talking about their problem somewhere online. Your job is to find those places. This is not about spying. It is about understanding where the conversations happen so you can eventually participate in them.

Go looking in:

**Reddit.** Search for subreddits related to your product category. If you are selling dog products, subreddits like r/dogs, r/puppy101, or r/dogtraining have thousands of people discussing exactly the problems your product might solve. Read the posts. Note the language people use and the frustrations they describe.

**Facebook groups.** Search for groups around your customer's interest or situation. "First-time dog owners UK" or "small car hacks" or whatever matches your niche. These groups are goldmines for understanding how people think about the problem.

**YouTube comments.** Find videos related to your product category and read the comments. People asking questions in YouTube comments are often in buying mode. They are actively looking for solutions.

**Forum and review sections.** Niche forums, Trustpilot reviews of competing products, Amazon Q&A sections. Anywhere your customer is expressing opinions, asking questions, or describing frustrations.

Write down three to five specific places where your customer gathers. You will use these in Chapter 11 when you start driving traffic, so the work you do now pays off directly later.

## What they value most

Different customers buy for different reasons, even when they are buying the same type of product. Understanding what your customer values most helps you position your offer (Chapter 8) and write your listing (Chapter 9) in a way that speaks directly to their priorities.

The most common purchase drivers:

**Price.** They want the best deal. They compare multiple options and go with the lowest cost that seems acceptable. If this is your customer, your listing needs to communicate value clearly and your pricing needs to be competitive.

**Convenience.** They want the easiest solution. Fast shipping, simple setup, minimal hassle. They will pay a bit more to avoid complexity. If this is your customer, your listing should emphasise how easy the product is to use, how fast it arrives, and how little effort is involved.

**Quality and durability.** They have been burned by cheap alternatives and want something that lasts. They are willing to pay more for reliability. If this is your customer, your listing needs specifics: materials, construction details, lifespan, and ideally evidence (reviews, testing results, guarantees).

**Uniqueness.** They want something that feels special, different, or personalised. Mass-market products bore them. If this is your customer, your listing should highlight what makes yours different and why it is not available everywhere.

**Problem solved completely.** They are frustrated and want the problem gone. They do not care about brand, aesthetics, or trends. They care about whether it works. If this is your customer, lead with the problem and the solution. Show them you understand their frustration and that your product fixes it.

Most customers have a primary driver and one or two secondary ones. Identify your customer's primary driver and build your messaging around it.

[IMAGE: Five simple icons in a horizontal row, each representing a purchase driver. Left to right: a price tag (Price), a lightning bolt (Convenience), a shield (Quality), a star or fingerprint (Uniqueness), a checkmark in a circle (Problem solved). Each icon has a one-line label beneath it. Clean, minimal style. This serves as a quick visual reference the learner can come back to.]

## What would make them trust a new seller?

This is a question most new sellers forget to ask, and it is one of the most important ones.

Your customer has never heard of you. They have no reason to trust you. Every other option they are considering has reviews, a track record, or brand recognition that you do not have yet.

So what would make them take a chance?

**Specificity.** Vague listings feel untrustworthy. Specific details (exact dimensions, materials, weight, what is included) signal that you know your product and care about accuracy.

**Honesty about limitations.** If your product does not suit every use case, say so. "This cover fits hatchbacks and small SUVs. For larger vehicles, check our measurement guide." A seller who acknowledges limitations feels more trustworthy than one who claims their product is perfect for everyone.

**Responsive communication.** Answering questions quickly and helpfully builds trust fast. On marketplaces, your response time is often visible to potential buyers. Being responsive says "this is a real person who cares about their customers."

**A clear returns policy.** Nothing reduces purchase anxiety like knowing they can return it if it is not right. A fair, visible returns policy does not increase returns. It increases purchases.

**Social proof, even early on.** You may not have reviews yet. But you can include lifestyle photos showing the product in use, specific details about where and how it is made, and a genuine about-you section that tells your story. People buy from people, especially when the alternative is a faceless Amazon listing.

You will build on all of these in Chapters 9 and 10. For now, write down what you think would make your specific customer trust a new seller enough to buy. This will shape your store, your listings, and your communication style.

## You now know who you are talking to

At the start of this chapter, you had a product. Now you have a product and a customer. You know who they are, where they spend time online, what they value, and what would make them trust you.

That combination is powerful. Most sellers skip this work entirely and write generic listings aimed at nobody in particular. You will not make that mistake.

Next up: turning your product and your customer profile into an offer that stands out. This is where your business starts to feel like yours.

# Chapter 8 — Shape Your Offer

**Estimated reading time:** 10 minutes
**Worksheet:** Offer worksheet
**Canvas connection:** Value Proposition and Solution sections populate. Cross-check: does price minus unit economics (Ch5) leave viable margin?

---

## A product is not an offer

You have a product that the numbers support and a clear picture of who will buy it. That is a strong foundation. But a product sitting on a shelf is not yet a business. An offer is.

Here is the difference. A product is a thing. An offer is the complete package that makes someone choose your thing over every other option available to them. It includes the product, but also the price, the promise, the way you present it, and the reason it feels right for this specific buyer.

Two sellers can sell an identical physical product and have completely different results because their offers are different. One wraps it in generic packaging with a bland description. The other positions it for a specific customer, prices it confidently, describes it in language that resonates, and backs it with a clear promise. Same product. Very different businesses.

This chapter helps you build the second version.

## What is your customer doing right now?

Before you can shape a compelling offer, you need to understand what your customer's current alternative is. Because you are not competing against nothing. You are competing against whatever they are doing today to deal with the problem.

There are usually three possibilities:

**They are buying a competitor's product.** They already spend money on a solution, but they are not completely happy with it (you found this in Chapter 3 when you read their reviews). Your offer needs to be clearly better in a way they care about.

**They are using a workaround.** They have cobbled together a DIY solution or are making do with something that was not designed for this purpose. Your offer needs to be more convenient or more effective than their workaround, at a price that feels fair for the upgrade.

**They are ignoring the problem.** They have not solved it because nothing available seemed worth the money or effort. Your offer needs to make the solution feel easy, accessible, and obviously worth it.

Knowing which situation your customer is in changes how you talk about your product. If they are buying from a competitor, you focus on what makes yours better. If they are using a workaround, you focus on the upgrade. If they are ignoring the problem, you focus on how simple the solution is.

## Position against the alternatives

[TOOLTIP: Positioning | How your product sits in the customer's mind relative to the other options available to them. Strong positioning makes it clear who your product is for, what makes it different, and why it is the right choice for that specific buyer. It is not a slogan. It is a strategic decision about where you stand in the market.] is not about being better at everything. It is about being clearly better at the thing your customer cares about most.

Look back at the purchase drivers from Chapter 7. You identified what your customer values most. Your positioning should lead with that.

If they value durability and you sell a higher-quality version of a product known for breaking, your positioning is: "Built to last when the cheap alternatives will not."

If they value convenience and you sell a product that solves a problem with minimal setup, your positioning is: "Ready in minutes, not hours."

If they value price and you offer a solid product at a fair price without the brand markup, your positioning is: "Everything you need, nothing you are paying extra for."

You do not need to win on every dimension. Pick the one or two things that matter most to your customer and own them completely.

[IMAGE: A simple visual showing a product in the centre with three or four competitor products around it, each labelled with a single word (e.g. "Cheapest", "Most popular", "Premium"). The central product has a label showing its specific positioning, e.g. "Most durable for small spaces." Arrows or lines connect the central product to the competitor it most directly challenges. The visual should feel like a strategic map, showing that positioning is about choosing your territory, not trying to occupy all of them.]

## The minimum viable offer

There is a strong temptation to over-build your first offer. Custom packaging. Multiple product variants. A loyalty programme. A beautifully designed brand guide. Matching social media templates.

Resist this.

Your first offer needs to be good enough to sell, not perfect enough to win a design award. Every hour and pound you spend on polish before you have consistent sales is a resource taken away from learning what actually works.

Ask yourself: what is the simplest version of this offer that still solves the customer's problem and feels credible?

For most products, that is:
- One product (not a range)
- One or two variants at most (if variants are necessary)
- Clear, honest packaging (clean and functional, not premium)
- A strong listing (you will build this in Chapter 9)
- A fair, competitive price

That is it. You can add complexity, variants, bundles, and branding as you learn what your customers respond to. But start simple so that every improvement you make is informed by real customer behaviour, not guesswork.

**A useful principle:** launch version one, learn from real customers, then build version two. Version two will be better than anything you could have designed in isolation because it will be shaped by evidence.

## Name it and describe it

You need two things: a working product name and a positioning statement.

**The product name** does not need to be clever or brandable right now. It needs to be clear and searchable. "Heavy-Duty Waterproof Dog Seat Cover" works perfectly at this stage. You can develop branding later when you know what resonates with your customers.

If you are private labelling or building a brand, you will eventually want a brand name and product names that work together. For now, clarity beats creativity.

**The positioning statement** is one sentence that captures what you sell, who it is for, and why they should care. Here is a simple structure:

"[Product] for [customer] who need [key benefit] — [differentiator]."

Examples:

"Heavy-duty seat cover for dog owners with small cars who need something that fits without taking over the back seat — waterproof, compact, and machine washable."

"Replacement vacuum filters for [specific model] owners who are tired of aftermarket filters that do not fit — precision cut for a perfect seal, every time."

"Weekly planner templates for freelancers who struggle to balance client work and their own projects — designed around how freelance weeks actually work, not a corporate 9-to-5."

Your positioning statement is not a tagline for your website. It is a tool for your own clarity. Every decision you make about your listing, your pricing, your photos, and your marketing should be consistent with this sentence. If something contradicts it, one of them needs to change.

## Price it with confidence

You already know your unit economics from Chapter 5. You know what the product costs you and what margin you keep at different price points. Now you need to set the price that goes on the listing.

Pricing feels emotional but it is actually a straightforward decision when you have the right information:

**Your floor:** the minimum price that gives you a viable margin after all costs. You calculated this in Chapter 5. Below this number, you lose money. Do not go below your floor.

**Your ceiling:** the highest price your customer would consider reasonable. This is informed by what competitors charge (from Chapter 3) and what your customer values (from Chapter 7). Going above the ceiling means fewer sales, but each sale is more profitable.

**Your sweet spot:** somewhere between the floor and ceiling where the margin is healthy and the price feels fair relative to the alternatives. This is usually where your offer's positioning justifies the price.

If your product is positioned as the most durable option in a category full of cheap products that break, pricing slightly above the average makes sense. It reinforces your positioning. If your product is positioned as the smart budget choice, pricing near the lower end makes sense.

One thing to be honest about: you will not get the price perfectly right on your first attempt. That is fine. Price is one of the easiest things to adjust based on real-world results. Set it where you think it should be, test, and refine.

[IMAGE: A simple horizontal price scale showing three markers. Left marker: "Your floor — minimum viable margin" (with a small warning icon). Right marker: "Your ceiling — most the customer would pay" (with a small limit icon). Middle marker: "Your sweet spot" (highlighted, with a small target icon). Between floor and ceiling, a shaded zone shows the viable pricing range. The visual should feel like a helpful tool, not a complicated chart.]

## Check: does your margin still work?

Now that you have set a price, run the unit economics one more time. Take the worksheet from Chapter 5 and plug in your final selling price. After all costs, what is your margin per sale?

This margin needs to be large enough to eventually cover the cost of acquiring customers through advertising (you will learn the specifics in Chapter 12). As a rough early benchmark: if your margin per sale is at least 40% of the selling price, you are in a reasonable position. If it is below 25%, you may find it difficult to advertise profitably.

If the margin looks thin at your chosen price, you have a few options: negotiate better terms with your supplier, reduce packaging or shipping costs, adjust the product specification, or increase the price and rely on stronger positioning to justify it.

The canvas in the background is already connecting these numbers. Your margin from this chapter, your money cap from Chapter 4, and your unit economics from Chapter 5 all feed into the same picture. If something does not add up, the earlier you spot it the better.

[LOOP: If your margins do not work at any price the customer would accept, this idea may not be viable as a standalone product. That does not mean the customer or the category is wrong — it might mean the specific product needs to change. Could you offer a smaller version, a different material, a bundle, or a digital complement that improves the economics? If not, revisit your shortlist from Chapter 5. You have done the hardest part of the work already. Applying it to a new candidate is quick.]

## Your offer is ready

Look at what you have built across Chapters 2 through 8. You chose a sourcing model. You brainstormed ideas using demand evidence. You set operating rules to protect your time and money. You ran the numbers and eliminated the ideas that could not make money. You tested on a marketplace and got real results. You identified your customer in detail. And now you have a positioned, priced offer ready to sell.

That is not a plan on a page. That is a real business taking shape.

The next phase is about getting your store ready and writing listings that do the selling for you. You already have all the raw material: a product, a customer, a price, and a positioning statement. Now you are going to put it all together in a way that makes people buy.

# Chapter 9 — Write Listings That Sell

## Your listing is your salesperson

In a physical shop, a real person greets the customer, answers their questions, handles their objections, and helps them feel confident enough to buy. In your online store, the product listing does all of that.

A great product with a weak listing will sit unsold. A decent product with a strong listing will outsell it every time. The good news is that writing a strong listing is a learnable skill, not a talent you are born with. And you already have most of the raw material from the work you did in Chapters 7 and 8.

Your customer profile tells you who you are writing for. Your positioning statement tells you what to emphasise. Your competitive research tells you what existing listings are missing. This chapter turns all of that into a listing that does the selling while you sleep.

## Write a title that gets found and gets clicked

Your product title has two jobs. First, it needs to contain the words people actually search for so the platform shows it in results. Second, it needs to be compelling enough that someone chooses to click on yours instead of the listings above and below it.

**Start with the search terms.** Go back to your research from Chapter 3. What words did customers use when describing this type of product? What terms appear in the bestselling listings? Those are the words that belong in your title.

"Waterproof Dog Car Seat Cover" will appear in searches for "dog car seat cover" and "waterproof seat cover." That is good. "The Ultimate Canine Travel Companion" will appear in almost no searches because nobody types those words when they are looking to buy.

**Then add specificity.** After the core search terms, add the details that help the right buyer recognise this is for them. Size, key feature, material, compatibility.

Here are some before and after examples:

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Dog Seat Cover | Waterproof Dog Car Seat Cover — Universal Fit, Heavy-Duty, Machine Washable |
| Phone Case | Slim Silicone Phone Case for iPhone 15 — Drop Protection, Matte Black |
| Planner | Weekly Planner for Freelancers — Undated, Project Tracking, A5 Size |

The "after" versions are longer and more specific. They tell the buyer exactly what the product is, who it is for, and what makes it worth clicking on. Every word earns its place.

**What to leave out of the title:** your brand name (unless you are already well known, it takes up space without adding value), subjective claims like "best" or "amazing" (buyers ignore these), and information that belongs in the description (full feature lists, lengthy measurements).

## Write a description that answers every question

A buyer who clicks on your listing is interested. Your description's job is to move them from interested to confident. The way to do that is simple: answer every question they could possibly have before they need to ask it.

**Lead with the problem and the solution.** Do not start with a list of features. Start with the reason this product exists.

"Tired of cheap seat covers that slide around, soak through, or fall apart after a month? This cover was designed for dog owners who need something that actually stays put, actually repels water, and actually lasts."

That opening does something a feature list cannot. It tells the buyer "I understand your frustration and I built this to fix it." It speaks their language because you learned that language in Chapters 3 and 7.

**Then cover the specifics.** After the opening, give them everything they need to make a decision:

- What the product does and how it works
- Exact dimensions, weight, and material
- What is included (and what is not)
- Compatibility information (sizes, models, fit)
- Care instructions
- Shipping details and expected delivery time

**Address objections before they arise.** Think about the reasons someone might hesitate. Common objections for physical products include: "Will it fit?" "Is it easy to install?" "What if it does not work for me?" "How long will it last?"

Build the answers into your description. "Fits all standard car back seats from hatchbacks to SUVs. Installs in under 60 seconds with adjustable headrest straps. If it does not fit your car, return it within 30 days for a full refund."

Every objection you answer in the listing is a customer you do not lose at checkout.

[IMAGE: A side-by-side comparison of two product descriptions. Left side labelled "Feature dump" shows a bulleted list of dry specifications with no context: "100% polyester. 140cm x 120cm. Black. Waterproof coating." Right side labelled "Sells the product" shows the same information woven into benefit-led copy: "Made from heavy-duty polyester with a waterproof backing that stops muddy paw prints reaching your seats. Sized at 140cm x 120cm to fit standard back seats without bunching or sliding." The right side should feel warmer and more convincing while containing the same factual information.]

## Take photos that build confidence

Online, the buyer cannot touch, hold, or try your product. Your photos are the closest thing they have to a physical experience. They need to answer the question: "What am I actually getting?"

You do not need a professional camera or a studio. A modern smartphone and five minutes of setup will produce photos that look clean and trustworthy.

**Natural light is your best tool.** Position your product near a window during daylight. Avoid direct sunlight, which creates harsh shadows. Overcast days or indirect light from a north-facing window give the softest, most even lighting. Never use your phone's flash for product photos. It flattens the image and creates a cheap-looking glare.

**Use a clean background.** A sheet of white paper or card behind and beneath the product is all you need for a clean product shot. Prop the paper against a wall so it curves gently from the background to the surface, creating a seamless look. No wrinkles, no clutter, no distracting objects.

**Take more angles than you think you need.** For every product, aim for:

- Front view (the main listing image)
- Back view
- Close-up of key details (texture, stitching, label, mechanism)
- Scale shot (the product next to a common object or in someone's hand so the buyer understands the size)
- In-use shot (the product being used in its intended context)

The in-use shot is particularly powerful. A seat cover photographed flat on a white background looks like a piece of fabric. The same cover installed in a car with a dog sitting on it tells a story the buyer can see themselves in.

[IMAGE: A four-panel grid showing the same product photographed four ways. Panel 1: "Clean product shot" — product on white background, well-lit. Panel 2: "Detail shot" — close-up of a key feature like stitching or material texture. Panel 3: "Scale shot" — product next to a hand or everyday object. Panel 4: "In-use shot" — product being used in context (e.g. installed in a car, being worn, on a desk). Each panel has a small label. The overall message: "These four photos are all you need to start, and you can take them all with your phone."]

**Common mistakes to avoid:**

- Photographing on your kitchen table, bed, or carpet (backgrounds distract and look unprofessional)
- Using filters or heavy editing (the product should look like itself, not a version of itself)
- Only showing one angle (buyers want to see what they are getting from every side)
- Forgetting scale (a product that looks large in the photo but arrives small leads to returns and bad reviews)

## Social proof when you are starting from zero

Here is something important to understand about online reviews: people are significantly more likely to leave a review when they are unhappy than when they are happy. A satisfied customer uses the product and moves on with their life. A dissatisfied customer feels compelled to warn others.

This means that early reviews are disproportionately likely to be negative, not because your product is bad, but because the review system is weighted toward complaints. Knowing this helps you plan for it rather than being blindsided by it.

**How to encourage happy customers to leave reviews:**

The most effective approach is simple: ask. A follow-up message 7 to 14 days after delivery, when the customer has had time to use the product, with a direct and friendly request. "Hi, I hope you are enjoying [product]. If you have a moment, a quick review would mean a lot to a small business like ours. Thank you."

That is it. No bribes, no manipulation, no "leave a 5-star review and get a discount." Just a genuine, timely request. Most platforms allow you to send follow-up messages to buyers. Use this.

Some sellers include a small card in the package: "Thank you for your order. If you love it, we would really appreciate a quick review." This is a gentle, physical reminder that arrives at the moment of unboxing, which is usually when the customer feels most positive about their purchase.

**How to handle negative reviews:**

They will happen. Even excellent products get negative reviews. How you respond matters more than the review itself, because every future buyer will read your response.

**Do:** Respond quickly, politely, and constructively. Acknowledge the issue. Offer a solution (replacement, refund, or guidance). Show that you take customer satisfaction seriously. "I am sorry this did not meet your expectations. I would love to make it right. Could you message me directly so I can arrange a replacement or refund?"

**Do not:** Get defensive, argue with the reviewer, make excuses, or ignore it. A defensive response does more damage than the original review because it tells every future buyer that you do not handle problems well.

A negative review with a thoughtful, professional response actually builds trust. It shows potential buyers that if something goes wrong, you will sort it out. That is more reassuring than a page of five-star reviews with no seller interaction.

**Building trust before you have reviews:**

While you are building your review count, other elements of your listing do the trust-building work:

- Specific, detailed product information (signals expertise and honesty)
- Multiple high-quality photos (signals professionalism)
- A clear returns policy (reduces purchase risk)
- An "about" section that tells your story briefly and genuinely (people buy from people)
- Responsive communication when buyers ask questions

Trust is not built by one single thing. It is built by the accumulation of small signals that say: this is a real person selling a real product who cares about getting it right.

## Your listing is ready to work for you

You now have a product title built for search and clicks, a description that sells by answering every question, photos that build confidence, and a plan for gathering social proof.

This listing is not just a description of your product. It is your best salesperson, working around the clock, for every visitor who lands on your page.

Next up: setting up your store with everything a buyer needs to feel confident checking out. You have already done the hardest creative work. The next chapter is about assembling the pieces.

## It is simpler than you think

If you have been putting off setting up your store because it feels like a big, technical project, this chapter is going to be a relief.

Modern e-commerce platforms are designed for people who are not technical. Shopify, WooCommerce, Squarespace, Big Cartel, Etsy — every one of them walks you through the setup process with guided steps, templates, and built-in tools. You do not write code. You fill in forms, choose options, and upload the content you already created in Chapter 9.

If you have ever set up a social media profile, created a document in Google Docs, or posted something on Facebook, you have the skills to build a store. The platform handles the complicated bits (payment processing, security, mobile responsiveness) and you handle the parts that make it yours.

This chapter is not going to tell you which buttons to click in a specific platform. Every platform has its own free setup guides for that, and those guides are kept up to date in a way this course cannot match. What this chapter does is tell you what your store needs to have, regardless of which platform you choose, so you can follow any setup guide with clarity and confidence.

## Choose your platform

If you have not already chosen, here is a simple framework:

**Shopify** is the most popular choice for a good reason. It handles everything (hosting, payments, design) in one package. Monthly fee starts around £25 to £30. Ideal if you want a standalone branded store and plan to drive your own traffic.

**Etsy** is a marketplace, not a standalone store. You benefit from their existing buyer traffic, which is valuable when starting out. Best for handmade, creative, vintage, or unique products. Lower upfront cost but they take a percentage of each sale.

**WooCommerce** is a free plugin for WordPress. More control and no monthly platform fee, but you handle hosting and more of the technical setup. Best if you are comfortable with WordPress or have someone who can help.

**Big Cartel** is simpler and cheaper than Shopify, with a free tier for up to five products. Good for artists, makers, and very small catalogues.

**Amazon** gives you access to an enormous buyer base but is the most competitive environment. Best for products with strong search demand where you can compete on price, reviews, or unique features.

There is no wrong choice. Pick the one that fits your budget, your product type, and your comfort level. You can always migrate later. The selling skills you are building transfer across every platform.

## The essential building blocks

Every online store needs these elements. Think of this as your store's skeleton. Without any one of them, something feels off and the buyer's confidence drops.

### Your product page

This is the most important page in your store and you have already built it. Your listing from Chapter 9 — the title, description, photos, and pricing — becomes your product page. Upload it, format it according to your platform's layout, and make sure everything displays correctly on both desktop and mobile.

Check your product page on your phone before you consider it done. More than half of online shopping happens on mobile devices. If your photos are cropped oddly, your description is unreadable, or the buy button is hard to find on a small screen, you will lose sales.

### Your about page

Buyers want to know there is a real person behind the store. Your about page does not need to be long or polished. It needs to answer three questions: Who are you? Why did you start this? Why should I buy from you?

A few honest paragraphs are more effective than a corporate-sounding mission statement. "I started this store because I could not find a dog seat cover that actually fit my small car. After testing a dozen options that all slid around, fell apart, or did not fit, I decided to source one that actually works." That is relatable, specific, and builds trust.

### Contact information

The buyer needs to be able to reach you. At minimum, provide an email address. A contact form is even better. If you are comfortable with it, a phone number or live chat builds additional trust, but it is not required when starting out.

What matters is that the buyer can see a clear way to get in touch and that you respond promptly when they do. Being responsive is one of the easiest ways to stand out from competitors who ignore customer questions.

### Your returns and refund policy

A clear, visible returns policy is not just good practice. It is one of the most effective conversion tools you have. Buyers are more likely to purchase when they know they can return the product if it is not right. Counterintuitively, a generous returns policy does not usually increase returns. It increases purchases.

Decide on the basics:

- **Return window:** 14 days or 30 days are both standard. 30 days feels more generous and costs you very little extra in practice.
- **Who pays return shipping:** You can ask the buyer to pay (cheaper for you, slightly more friction) or offer free returns (more expensive but removes all purchase hesitation). Start with buyer-pays and consider upgrading to free returns once you have steady revenue.
- **Refund or exchange:** Offering both gives the buyer maximum flexibility.
- **Condition requirements:** "Unused and in original packaging" is standard and reasonable.

Write your policy in plain, friendly language. Avoid legal jargon. "If it is not right, send it back within 30 days and we will give you a full refund. We just ask that it is in its original condition" is clearer and more trustworthy than three paragraphs of formal terms.

### Payment processing

Your platform will guide you through setting up payments. Most offer [TOOLTIP: Stripe | A payment processing service that handles credit and debit card transactions for online stores. It integrates with most e-commerce platforms and takes a small percentage (typically around 2-3%) of each transaction. You do not need to understand the technical details — your platform connects to Stripe for you.] and PayPal integration. Offering both gives your buyer the widest range of options.

PayPal in particular is worth enabling because many buyers feel safer using it, especially when buying from a store they have not purchased from before. It adds a layer of trust because the buyer knows PayPal will protect them if something goes wrong.

Setup is usually a matter of connecting your accounts. The platform walks you through it.

### Basic legal pages

Most platforms provide templates for a privacy policy and terms of service. Use them. You can customise them later, but the template versions are sufficient to start.

These pages exist because they are legally required in most regions and because they signal professionalism. A store without a privacy policy feels incomplete, even if the buyer never actually reads it.

### A working checkout flow

Before you launch, go through the entire buying process yourself. Add a product to the cart, go to checkout, and make sure everything works. If your platform lets you place a test order, do it. If not, ask a friend to buy something (you can refund them) and watch the process from their perspective.

Check for: Does the checkout load quickly? Are the shipping options clear? Does the payment go through? Does the order confirmation email send? Does the order appear in your dashboard?

Catching a broken checkout flow before your first real customer finds it is worth the five minutes this takes.

[IMAGE: A simple checklist visual styled as a friendly card or note, showing the store essentials with checkboxes: Product page (with photos, description, price) ✓, About page ✓, Contact information ✓, Returns/refund policy ✓, Payment processing ✓, Privacy policy and terms ✓, Checkout tested ✓. The visual should feel like a satisfying completion list, not a daunting to-do. Clean, warm, with a "you are almost there" energy.]

## Test your store with a real person

Before you open to the public, ask someone you trust — a friend, a family member, a partner — to go through your store as if they were a real customer. Ask them to:

- Find the product from the homepage
- Read the listing and tell you if anything is confusing
- Go through checkout (you can refund them afterwards)
- Tell you honestly: would they feel confident buying from this store?

Fresh eyes catch things you cannot see because you have been staring at it for too long. A confusing navigation menu, a typo in the product description, a photo that does not load, a shipping option that does not make sense. These small things erode trust and you want to fix them before real customers arrive.

Listen to their feedback without defending your choices. If something confused them, it will confuse your customers. Fix it.

## What "ready" actually means

Your store does not need to be perfect. It needs to be functional, trustworthy, and clear. A buyer should be able to land on your store, understand what you sell, find the product, feel confident enough to buy, and check out without friction.

That is the bar. You can (and should) improve it over time as you learn what works. But waiting for perfection before launching is one of the most common ways new sellers stall. A live store that is 80% polished teaches you more in one week than a store you are still tweaking in private after three months.

[LOOP: If something in this checklist is blocking you and you cannot figure it out, search for your specific platform's help documentation or community forum. Every major platform has extensive guides and active communities where people help each other with setup questions. You are not the first person to hit this snag, and the answer is almost certainly already written down somewhere. Do not let a single technical hiccup stop your momentum.]

## Your store is ready for customers

Think about where you are. You have a tested product with proven demand. You know your customer. You have a positioned offer with healthy margins. You have a listing that answers every question. And now you have a store that is set up, tested, and ready to take orders.

Most people who talk about starting an online business never get to this point. You are here.

The next phase is the exciting one: getting real customers to your store. You are going to start with free traffic methods that cost nothing but your time, and then learn how to test paid advertising with small budgets. The groundwork is laid. It is time to open the doors.

# Chapter 11 — Free Traffic: Start Without Spending

## Your store is open. Now people need to find it.

You have a live store with a strong listing, solid product photography, and a clear offer. That is more than most aspiring sellers ever achieve. But a store without visitors is like a shop on a street with no footfall. The product could be perfect and nobody would know.

This chapter teaches you how to bring your first visitors and your first sales without spending a penny on advertising. That matters for two reasons. First, it builds your confidence with real customer interactions before any money is at stake. Second, and this is important, it tests whether your listing actually converts visitors into buyers. If your listing does not convert free traffic, paying for traffic will just mean losing money faster.

Free traffic takes more time and patience than paid ads. But the visitors you attract through these methods are often more engaged because they found you through genuine interest, not because an ad interrupted their scrolling.

## Share in communities (without being the person everyone ignores)

Remember the places where your customer gathers that you identified in Chapter 7? Reddit threads, Facebook groups, hobbyist forums, YouTube comment sections. Those communities are where your first customers are already spending time.

The temptation is to join these spaces and immediately post about your product. Do not do this. Communities have an immune system. They can spot someone who joined purely to sell, and they will ignore you, call you out, or get you removed.

The approach that works is slower but far more effective:

**First, become a genuine member.** Spend a week or two participating. Answer questions. Share useful information. Help people. Contribute something that has nothing to do with your product. This is not a trick. It is how trust works in communities. People listen to members they recognise, not to strangers who show up with a sales pitch.

**Then, mention your product when it is genuinely relevant.** When someone asks a question your product solves, you have earned the right to mention it. "I actually built a product that does exactly this" from a recognised community member gets a completely different response than the same sentence from a brand new account.

**Be transparent.** If the community has rules about self-promotion (most do), follow them. If you mention your product, be upfront that it is yours. "Full disclosure, this is my product, but I think it is relevant to what you are asking" is honest and usually well received.

A single genuine recommendation from a trusted community member can drive more sales than a dozen promotional posts that everyone scrolls past.

## Basic SEO: help people find you when they search

[TOOLTIP: SEO (Search Engine Optimisation) | The practice of making your store and product pages easier for search engines like Google to find and show to people who are searching for what you sell. At its simplest, it means using the same words your customers type into Google when they are looking for your type of product.]

SEO sounds technical but at this stage it comes down to one principle: use the words your customer uses.

When someone types "waterproof dog seat cover small car" into Google, Google looks for pages that contain those words. If your product title, product description, and page titles contain those terms, your page is more likely to appear in the results.

You have already done most of this work. In Chapter 9 you wrote a product title using the search terms your customer uses and a description that naturally includes the language they speak. That is SEO in practice.

A few additional things you can do:

**Your page titles and descriptions.** Every page on your store has a title tag and a meta description that appear in Google search results. Most platforms let you customise these. Use them to include your key search terms in a natural sentence. "Waterproof Dog Seat Cover for Small Cars — Heavy Duty, Machine Washable" is a page title that works for both Google and the human reading it.

**Image file names and alt text.** When you upload product photos, name the files descriptively. "waterproof-dog-seat-cover-installed.jpg" is better than "IMG_4392.jpg." Most platforms also let you add [TOOLTIP: alt text | A short description of an image that helps search engines understand what the image shows. It is also read aloud by screen readers for visually impaired users. Write it as a simple description: "Waterproof dog seat cover installed in a hatchback with a golden retriever sitting on it."] to each image. Use it. It helps search engines understand your images and it helps your pages appear in image search results.

**Your about page and other content.** Any text on your store is an opportunity to include relevant terms naturally. Your about page might mention the type of products you sell, the customers you serve, and the problems you solve. This is not about stuffing keywords everywhere. It is about making sure the words on your site match the words people search for.

SEO results take time. You will not appear on the first page of Google tomorrow. But every piece of content you create with the right language is a small investment that compounds over weeks and months. Some of your most reliable long-term traffic will eventually come from people finding you through search.

## Social media as distribution, not a branding project

You do not need a content calendar, a posting schedule across five platforms, or a carefully curated brand aesthetic. Not right now. That comes later, if it comes at all.

Right now, social media is a distribution channel. You are using it to put your product in front of people who might want it. That is the entire goal.

**Pick one platform.** The one where your customer spends time (you identified this in Chapter 7). If your audience is on Instagram, use Instagram. If they are on TikTok, use TikTok. If they are on Pinterest, use Pinterest. Do not spread yourself across multiple platforms. One platform, done consistently, beats three platforms done poorly.

**Show the product in context.** The most effective social media content for a small product business is not polished brand imagery. It is the product being used. A 15-second video of your seat cover being installed. A photo of your planner open on a desk with a coffee beside it. A before-and-after showing the problem and the solution. Real, simple, useful content.

**Post consistently at a sustainable pace.** Three posts a week for 12 weeks is better than daily posts for 2 weeks followed by silence. Check your time budget from Chapter 4 and be realistic. If you can commit to three posts a week, commit to that and maintain it.

**Engage with the responses.** Reply to every comment and every message. The algorithm on most platforms rewards engagement, so active conversations make your content more visible. But more importantly, these early interactions are conversations with potential customers. They will tell you what they like, what they are unsure about, and what would make them buy.

## Your personal network (used respectfully)

Your friends, family, and colleagues can help you get started. But there is a right way and a wrong way to ask.

**The wrong way:** A broadcast post on your personal social media begging everyone to buy your product and support your dream. This creates guilt-based purchases from people who do not need what you sell, generates no useful feedback, and makes people avoid your posts.

**The right way:** A direct, specific message to people who might genuinely know someone in your target audience. "I have just launched a seat cover designed for dog owners with smaller cars. You probably do not need one, but if you know anyone who might, I would really appreciate you sending them the link."

This works because it is honest, it is specific, and it asks for a referral rather than a pity purchase. A referral from a friend carries real trust. And the person receiving the recommendation actually needs the product, which means they might become a genuine repeat customer.

## Track what is working

As traffic starts coming in, pay attention to where it comes from. Most store platforms show you basic [TOOLTIP: traffic sources | Where your visitors come from. Your store dashboard will typically break this down into categories like direct (typed your URL), social (came from a social media platform), search (found you on Google), and referral (clicked a link on another website). Knowing which source drives the most visitors and sales helps you focus your effort where it matters.] in their analytics dashboard.

You do not need to obsess over numbers at this stage. But knowing that your Reddit comments drive 10 times more traffic than your Instagram posts is valuable information. It tells you where to focus your limited time for maximum impact.

Check your traffic sources once a week during your weekly review (from Chapter 4). Note which channels are driving visitors and, more importantly, which channels are driving buyers. Some sources bring lots of browsers. Others bring fewer visitors but more purchases. The second type is more valuable.

## Free traffic takes patience, and that is fine

You may not see results in the first week. That is normal. Communities take time to build trust in. SEO takes time to index. Social media takes time to find its audience.

The consistency principle from your Founder Rules applies here. Small, regular effort over weeks will build a steady stream of visitors. One community where you are a valued member. One social media platform where you post reliably. One set of product pages that are optimised for search.

These channels compound. The Reddit comment you post today might drive traffic for months. The blog post or social media content you create now will keep working long after you publish it. That is the beauty of free traffic: once it is built, it keeps delivering without ongoing cost.

And the traffic you build now serves another purpose. It tells you whether your listing converts. If 100 people visit your product page and none of them buy, the issue is your listing, your price, or your offer, not your traffic. That is essential knowledge before you start spending money on ads in the next chapter.

## You are building momentum

You have a live store, a listing that sells, and now traffic flowing in. Some of those visitors are becoming customers. You are making real sales to real people who found you through genuine channels.

The next chapter teaches you how to accelerate this with paid advertising. Small budgets, simple tests, clear signals. You will learn the metrics that matter and how to read them. But the foundation you have built with free traffic means you are not going in blind. You already know your listing works. Paid ads simply put it in front of more of the right people.

# Chapter 12 — Paid Ads: Small Budget Testing

**Estimated reading time:** 12 minutes

## Now you can afford to accelerate

You have proven something important with free traffic: people visit your store and some of them buy. Your listing converts. Your offer works. That means putting more of the right people in front of that listing should mean more sales.

That is what paid advertising does. It puts your product in front of people who are likely to be interested, on platforms they already use, at a pace you control.

This chapter is not about becoming a marketing expert. It is about running a simple, small-budget test to find out whether paid advertising can profitably bring you customers. You will learn what the key numbers mean, how to set up your first test, and how to read the results using the decision rules you set in Chapter 4.

## How paid ads actually work

When you run an ad on a platform like Meta (Facebook and Instagram), Google, TikTok, or Pinterest, you are entering an auction. You are telling the platform: "Here is my ad. Here is the type of person I want to see it. Here is my daily budget." The platform then shows your ad to people who match your targeting and charges you based on how many people see or interact with it.

You set a daily budget and the platform will not exceed it. If you set £5 per day, you will spend £5 per day. You can pause or stop at any time. This makes it very low risk to test, because you are in complete control of how much you spend.

**Which platform to start with:** For most physical products, Meta (Facebook and Instagram ads) is the best starting point. The targeting is sophisticated, the audience is enormous, and the ad creation tools are straightforward. Google Ads works well when people actively search for your type of product. TikTok and Pinterest are worth exploring later, but start with one platform and learn it before adding others.

[IMAGE: A simple flow diagram showing how paid ads work: "You create an ad" → "You choose your audience" → "You set a daily budget" → "Platform shows your ad to matching people" → "People click through to your store" → "Some of them buy." Each step is a small illustrated node connected by arrows. Clean, friendly, non-technical. The flow should feel simple and manageable, not like a complex system.]

## Set up your first test

Keep your first ad test deliberately simple. You are not trying to create a viral campaign. You are trying to answer one question: can paid ads bring you customers at a cost that makes sense for your business?

**Budget:** Start with £5 to £10 per day. At that level, a 7-day test costs between £35 and £70. That is enough to generate meaningful data without a significant financial commitment. Check that this fits within the test budget from your Founder Rules in Chapter 4.

**Duration:** Run the test for at least 7 days. Shorter tests do not produce enough data to draw conclusions. The platform's algorithm also needs 2 to 3 days to optimise who it shows your ad to, so the first few days are often not representative of long-term performance.

**Audience:** Use the customer profile from Chapter 7. Most ad platforms let you target by age, location, interests, and behaviours. Do not try to reach everyone. Target the specific type of person you identified as your customer. A well-targeted ad shown to 5,000 of the right people outperforms a broadly targeted ad shown to 50,000 random people.

**The ad itself:** Use your best product photo and a short, clear message. Lead with the benefit, not the features. "Finally, a dog seat cover that actually fits small cars" is more compelling than "Waterproof polyester seat cover, 140cm x 120cm." Include a clear call to action: "Shop now" or "See why dog owners are switching."

**Landing page:** Send the ad traffic directly to your product page, not to your homepage. The person clicked because they were interested in a specific product. Take them straight to it. Every extra click between the ad and the product page loses potential customers.

## The numbers that matter (and what they actually mean)

When your ad has been running for a few days, you will see numbers in the ads dashboard. Here is what each one means and why it matters:

**[TOOLTIP: Click-through rate (CTR) | The percentage of people who saw your ad and clicked on it. If 1,000 people saw your ad and 20 clicked, your CTR is 2%. It tells you whether your ad is grabbing attention. Above 1.5% is usually a positive sign. Below 1% suggests the ad image or message is not resonating with the audience.]**

Your CTR tells you whether your ad is interesting enough to make people want to learn more. If it is low, the issue is usually the image, the headline, or the audience targeting. The product might be great but the ad is not communicating that.

**[TOOLTIP: Cost per click (CPC) | How much you pay each time someone clicks on your ad. If you spent £10 and got 25 clicks, your CPC is £0.40. This varies widely depending on your product category and the competition for your audience. Lower is better, but CPC only matters in relation to what those clicks are worth to you.]**

Your CPC tells you how efficiently your budget is turning into store visitors. It is useful context but it is not the number that matters most. A CPC of £1.00 that leads to a sale worth £15 profit is better than a CPC of £0.10 where nobody buys.

**[TOOLTIP: Conversion rate | The percentage of people who visited your store and completed a purchase. If 100 people visited and 3 bought, your conversion rate is 3%. For a new store, 1% to 3% is a normal range. Below 1% suggests your listing, price, or product page needs improvement. Above 3% is strong.]**

Your conversion rate tells you whether your store turns visitors into buyers. This is partly the job of your listing (Chapter 9) and partly the job of your offer and price (Chapter 8). If people click the ad but do not buy, the ad is doing its job but the store is not.

**[TOOLTIP: Cost per acquisition (CPA) | The total amount you spent on ads divided by the number of sales those ads generated. If you spent £50 and got 3 sales, your CPA is £16.67. This is the most important number because it tells you how much each customer costs you. Compare it directly to your margin per sale from Chapter 5.]**

Your CPA is the number that connects advertising to profitability. It answers: how much does it cost to get one customer?

Here is where everything comes together. If your margin per sale (from Chapter 5) is £10 and your CPA is £8, you are making £2 profit on every sale from ads. If your CPA is £15, you are losing £5 on every sale. That is not sustainable unless your customers come back and buy again (which you will work on in Chapter 13).

[IMAGE: A simple visual equation showing how the numbers connect: "Margin per sale (from Ch.5)" minus "CPA (cost to get that sale)" equals "Profit per customer". Show two scenarios side by side. Left: Margin £10 minus CPA £8 = £2 profit (shown in green with a small positive icon). Right: Margin £10 minus CPA £15 = -£5 loss (shown in amber with a small warning icon). Below the second scenario: "This is only a problem if they never buy again — see Chapter 13." The visual should feel instructive, not alarming.]

## Read your results and decide

After 7 days, sit down with your numbers. This is the moment your Founder Rules from Chapter 4 were built for.

**Strong signal: CTR above 1.5% and CPA at or below your margin.**
Your ad is working. People are clicking and buying at a cost that makes sense. You can continue running this ad and consider gradually increasing your budget. This does not mean double it overnight. Increase by 20% to 30% and see if the numbers hold.

**Mixed signal: decent CTR but CPA above your margin.**
People are interested (they click) but something between the click and the purchase is losing them. This usually means the product page needs work (revisit Chapter 9), the price feels too high relative to expectations (revisit Chapter 8), or the audience targeting is slightly off (you are reaching interested people who are not quite the right buyer).

**Weak signal: low CTR regardless of other numbers.**
The ad is not getting attention. Try a different image, a different headline, or a different audience. This is the cheapest problem to fix because you can test new ad creative without changing anything about your product or store.

**No sales despite reasonable traffic.**
If your ad brought 200 or more visitors to your product page and nobody bought, the issue is almost certainly the product page, the price, or the offer itself. Go back to Chapters 8 and 9 and look at the page through your customer's eyes. Is the description compelling? Are the photos building confidence? Is the price competitive? Is the returns policy visible?

## Update your Founder Rules with real numbers

This is the moment you have been building toward since Chapter 4. You now have real data. Real numbers from real customers responding to real ads.

Go back to your Founder Rules worksheet and update your kill, continue, and escalation criteria with specific thresholds:

- "I will stop if CPA stays above £X for two consecutive weeks." (Where X is a number that makes you unprofitable.)
- "I will continue if CPA is trending downward week over week."
- "I will invest more when CPA is consistently below £X and conversion rate is stable." (Where X leaves you with comfortable profit per sale.)

These rules are no longer abstract. They are grounded in evidence from your own business. That is a powerful position to be in.

## Common beginner mistakes (easily avoided)

**Changing the ad after two days.** The algorithm needs time to optimise. Two days of data tells you almost nothing. Wait the full 7 days.

**Targeting too broadly.** "Everyone aged 18 to 65" is not a target audience. Use the customer profile from Chapter 7 and be specific.

**Targeting too narrowly.** An audience of 500 people is too small for the algorithm to work with. Aim for an audience size in the tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands, depending on your product and geography.

**Sending traffic to the homepage.** The customer clicked on an ad for a specific product. Send them to that product page. Every extra click you add to the journey loses potential buyers.

**Judging results by likes and comments.** An ad can get dozens of likes and zero sales. The only metric that matters is whether people are buying. Likes are nice. Sales pay the bills.

**Increasing budget too fast after early success.** If your first £50 test goes well, the instinct is to throw £500 at it immediately. Resist this. Scale gradually: 20% to 30% budget increases, then observe whether the numbers hold at the new level.

[LOOP: If your first ad test did not produce profitable results, that is not a signal to stop advertising. It is a signal to diagnose and adjust. Was the CTR low (ad problem) or was the conversion rate low (page or offer problem)? Most successful ad campaigns took several rounds of testing to find the combination that works. Each round costs you a small test budget and gives you real information. The alternative, guessing without data, is far more expensive in the long run.]

## You now have a customer acquisition engine

Think about what you have built. A free traffic foundation that brings steady visitors. A paid advertising channel that you can dial up or down based on your budget and results. And clear numbers that tell you exactly what each customer costs and what each customer is worth.

That last part is rare. Most small businesses have no idea what it costs them to acquire a customer. You do. And that knowledge will shape every decision you make from here.

The next chapter adds the third piece of the revenue puzzle: getting the customers you have already paid to acquire to come back and buy again. Because the cheapest customer is always the one you already have.

# Chapter 13 — Email and Repeat Customers

**Estimated reading time:** 10 minutes
**Worksheet:** Email and Retention Plan worksheet
**Canvas connection:** Revenue Streams section populates with single-purchase and projected repeat revenue. Cross-check: LTV vs CPA reframes advertising profitability.

## The most valuable customer already knows you

Every customer you have acquired so far, whether through free traffic or paid ads, cost you something. Time, money, or both. If that customer buys once and never returns, you need to find a brand new person for every future sale. That is expensive.

But if that customer buys a second time, the second purchase costs you almost nothing to generate. No ad spend. No community building. No time creating content. They already know you, they already trust you, and they already have your product. The revenue from their second purchase is almost entirely profit.

This is why repeat customers transform the economics of a small business. And email is the most reliable way to make it happen.

## Why email beats social media for revenue

You might have followers on Instagram or members of a Facebook group who engage with your content. That is valuable. But you do not own that audience. The platform decides who sees your posts through its algorithm, and that algorithm changes constantly. On most platforms, organic reach (the percentage of your followers who actually see what you post) is between 2% and 10%. For every 100 followers, as few as 2 might see your latest post.

Email is different. When you send an email, it lands in the inbox. There is no algorithm deciding whether to show it. [TOOLTIP: Open rate | The percentage of people on your email list who open a given email. For e-commerce emails, 15% to 25% is a healthy range. This is significantly higher than social media organic reach, which is why email consistently generates more revenue per subscriber than social media does per follower.] for e-commerce emails is typically 15% to 25%, which is vastly higher than social media reach.

More importantly, email subscribers have given you permission to contact them. They chose to be on your list. That makes them a warmer, more engaged audience than followers who might have clicked "follow" months ago and forgotten about you.

## Start collecting emails from day one

Do not wait until you have a large store or a big product range. Start collecting emails now, with whatever you have.

**On your store:** Most platforms let you add an email signup form. A pop-up that appears after a few seconds on site, or an embedded form on your homepage. The key is offering something in return for their email address, because "subscribe to our newsletter" is not compelling enough.

What works:

- A discount on their first order ("Get 10% off your first purchase")
- Early access to new products
- A useful piece of content related to your product ("Free guide: how to keep your car clean with a dog")

The discount approach is the most effective for most stores. Yes, it reduces your margin on the first sale. But that customer is now on your email list, which means you can bring them back for full-price purchases in the future.

**From your marketplace sales:** If you tested on eBay, Etsy, or another marketplace in Chapter 6 and made sales, some of those platforms allow you to communicate with buyers. A polite post-sale message inviting them to join your email list (especially if you are now launching your own store) can convert marketplace buyers into direct customers.

**What to use:** [TOOLTIP: Mailchimp | An email marketing platform commonly used by small businesses. It lets you build an email list, design emails, and set up automated sequences (emails that send automatically based on triggers like a new signup or a purchase). The free tier supports up to 500 subscribers, which is more than enough to start.] is a popular starting point with a generous free tier. Klaviyo is popular for e-commerce specifically. Many store platforms (like Shopify) also have built-in email features. Pick one and set it up. The tool matters far less than actually sending emails.

## Your first three emails

You do not need a complex email strategy. You need three emails that go out automatically to every new subscriber. Write them once and they work for every person who signs up.

**Email 1: Welcome and deliver your promise.** Send immediately after signup. If you promised a discount code, this is where they get it. Keep it warm and short. "Welcome. Here is your 10% off code: WELCOME10. Here is a quick look at what we sell and why we started." Include a link to your best product.

**Email 2: Your story.** Send 2 to 3 days after the first email. Tell them briefly why you started this business and what you care about. This is not a corporate mission statement. It is a genuine, short story that helps them see you as a real person. "I started [store name] because I spent months looking for a seat cover that actually fit my small car and kept finding the same flimsy options that slid off the seat. I figured if I was frustrated, other dog owners probably were too." People buy from people. This email builds the relationship.

**Email 3: The product, with a clear reason to buy.** Send 5 to 7 days after signup. Feature your main product with a benefit-led description (not a feature list). Address the most common hesitation your customers have. Include a clear link to buy. If they used their discount code already, great. If not, remind them it is still available.

These three emails run on autopilot. Every new subscriber receives them in sequence regardless of when they sign up. They build familiarity, trust, and a gentle nudge toward purchasing. And you only write them once.

[IMAGE: A simple visual showing three envelopes or email icons in a horizontal sequence with time markers between them. Email 1: "Welcome + discount" (Day 0). Email 2: "Your story" (Day 2-3). Email 3: "The product + reason to buy" (Day 5-7). Each email has a one-line summary beneath it. The visual should feel like a simple, achievable sequence, not a complex automation diagram. Arrows connecting them with "automatic" labels to show it runs without manual effort.]

## After the sale: turn a buyer into a returning customer

The purchase is not the end of the customer relationship. It is the beginning. What happens after someone buys determines whether they come back or disappear forever.

Most stores do nothing after the sale beyond a standard order confirmation and a shipping notification. That is a missed opportunity.

Here is a simple post-purchase sequence that keeps the relationship alive:

**Order confirmation (immediate).** Your platform sends this automatically, but you can customise it. Add a line of personality: "Thanks for your order. We are packing it up with care and it will be on its way shortly." Small touches make the experience feel personal.

**Shipping notification (when dispatched).** Include the tracking information and an estimated delivery window. "Your order is on its way. You can track it here: [link]. It should arrive by [date]."

**Follow-up (7 to 14 days after delivery).** This is the most important post-purchase email. Check in: "Hi [name], your [product] should have arrived by now. We hope you love it. If anything is not right, just reply to this email and we will sort it out." Then ask for a review: "If you have a moment, a quick review would mean the world to a small business like ours. [Link to leave a review]."

This email serves three purposes. It catches problems before they become bad reviews (because you offered to fix them first). It generates positive reviews from happy customers (because you asked). And it reminds the customer that you exist and that you care, which makes them more likely to buy again.

**Repeat purchase prompt (30 to 60 days later).** If your product has a natural replacement cycle or if you sell multiple products, send an email around the time they might be ready to buy again. "It has been about a month since your [product] arrived. If you are ready for a second one (or want to try [related product]), here is a link." If you want to offer a small loyalty incentive, this is the place: "As a thank you for being a returning customer, here is 10% off your next order."

## Customer lifetime value: the number that changes everything

[TOOLTIP: Customer lifetime value (LTV) | The total amount of money a customer spends with you over the entire time they remain your customer. If someone buys twice a year at £20 each time and stays a customer for 3 years, their LTV is £120. LTV is powerful because it tells you how much you can afford to spend acquiring a customer while still being profitable.]

Here is where the maths gets exciting.

In Chapter 12, you learned what it costs to acquire a customer (CPA). In Chapter 5, you calculated your margin per sale. If a customer only ever buys once, your profit is simply margin minus CPA.

But if a customer buys more than once, the picture changes dramatically.

Say your margin per sale is £10 and your CPA is £8. On the first sale, you make £2 profit. Modest.

But if that customer buys three times over the next year, your total margin is £30 (three purchases at £10 margin each). You only paid £8 to acquire them once. Your total profit from that customer is £22.

That means you could actually afford a CPA of up to £29 and still break even on that customer over a year. Suddenly, campaigns that looked unprofitable based on a single sale become highly profitable when repeat purchases are factored in.

This is why email and retention matter so much. They are not just "nice to have" additions to your marketing. They fundamentally change how much you can afford to spend on growth.

You do not have enough data to calculate precise lifetime value yet. But you can start tracking: how many of your customers buy a second time? How long between purchases? What is the average number of orders per customer? These numbers will become clearer over the next few weeks and months, and they will feed directly into your operating dashboard in Chapter 17.

## You now have three engines

Take a step back and look at what you have built:

**Engine 1: Free traffic.** Communities, social media, and SEO driving steady visitors at no cost.

**Engine 2: Paid advertising.** A tested, measured channel you can dial up or down based on your budget and results.

**Engine 3: Email and retention.** A system that brings existing customers back, increases their lifetime value, and makes your advertising more profitable.

These three engines work together. Free traffic tests your listing. Paid ads accelerate what is working. Email turns one-time buyers into repeat customers. Each engine makes the others more effective.

Most small online stores have one of these, maybe two. You have all three, and you understand how they connect. That is a significant competitive advantage, especially when you are just getting started.

The next phase is about reading your numbers, making evidence-based decisions, and growing with confidence. You have a real, functioning business. Now you learn how to make it better.

# Chapter 14 — Read Your Numbers

## The difference between guessing and knowing

You have a live store, traffic coming in from multiple channels, customers buying, and emails going out. There is a lot happening. The question is: how do you know what is actually working?

Most small store owners operate on feeling. "I think this week was okay." "It feels like ads are working." "I reckon Instagram is helping." Feelings are fine for choosing what to eat for lunch. They are not reliable for running a business.

This chapter teaches you to replace feelings with facts. Not complicated analytics. Not a wall of spreadsheets. Six numbers, checked once a week, that tell you everything you need to know at this stage of your business.

## The six numbers that matter right now

Your business generates a lot of data. Your store dashboard, your ad platform, your email tool, and your spreadsheet all have numbers competing for your attention. Most of them are noise. These six are signal.

**1. Traffic (visitors per week)**
How many people visited your store this week? This is the top of your [TOOLTIP: funnel | The journey a person takes from first discovering your store to making a purchase. It is called a funnel because many people enter at the top (they see your ad or visit your store) but only a fraction come out at the bottom (they buy). Understanding your funnel helps you identify where people are dropping off.]. If traffic drops, fewer people are discovering you. If traffic grows, your marketing channels are working.

Where to find it: your store platform's analytics dashboard.

**2. Conversion rate (percentage who bought)**
Of all the people who visited, what percentage actually purchased? You learned about this in Chapter 12, but now you are tracking it as a weekly habit. For a new store, 1% to 3% is normal. Consistently below 1% means something about your product page, price, or offer needs work.

Where to find it: your store platform's analytics dashboard. Some platforms calculate it for you. If yours does not, divide total orders by total visitors and multiply by 100.

**3. Average order value**
How much does the typical customer spend per order? If you sell one product at one price, this is straightforward. If you sell multiple products or offer bundles, this tells you whether customers are buying more than one item. A rising average order value means your product range or bundling strategy is working.

Where to find it: your store platform's analytics or your own records (total revenue divided by total orders).

**4. Cost per acquisition (CPA)**
How much did it cost in advertising to get each sale this week? You learned this in Chapter 12. Track it weekly because it fluctuates. A CPA that is rising week on week needs attention. A CPA that is stable or falling is a sign your ads are performing well.

Where to find it: your ad platform's dashboard (total spend divided by total purchases attributed to ads).

**5. Margin per sale**
After all costs (product, shipping, packaging, platform fees, payment processing), how much do you actually keep per sale? You calculated this in Chapter 5, but real-world margin can differ from your projection. Shipping costs might vary. You might have offered discounts. Returns eat into margin. Track the actual number, not the projected one.

Where to find it: your own spreadsheet or bookkeeping tool. This is the one number your platforms will not calculate for you. You need to work it out from your records.

**6. Email list growth**
How many new subscribers joined your list this week? Your email list is one of the most valuable assets your business is building. It is the channel you own completely, and as you saw in Chapter 13, it is the foundation for repeat purchases. A list that grows week on week is a business that is building long-term value.

Where to find it: your email platform's dashboard (Mailchimp, Klaviyo, or your platform's built-in tool).

## Where to find your numbers

Every number lives somewhere specific. Here is a quick reference:

Your **store platform** (Shopify, WooCommerce, Etsy, etc.) shows traffic, conversion rate, average order value, total revenue, and orders. Most have an analytics or reports section in the admin area.

Your **ad platform** (Meta Ads Manager, Google Ads, etc.) shows impressions, clicks, CTR, CPC, and conversions. Some also calculate CPA directly.

Your **email tool** (Mailchimp, Klaviyo, etc.) shows subscriber count, open rates, and click rates.

Your **own records** (spreadsheet or bookkeeping tool) show actual costs per product, real margins, and total expenses. This is the one you maintain yourself, and it is arguably the most important because it tells the truth that no platform dashboard captures: how much money you are actually making or losing.

If you set up a simple spreadsheet in Chapter 4 as part of your bookkeeping red-line rule, this is where it pays off. Every expense and every sale recorded from day one means you can calculate real margin at any time.

## What "good" looks like at your stage

It is natural to wonder whether your numbers are good or bad. Here are some benchmarks for an early-stage online store. These are not targets to hit. They are reference points so you know whether you are in a normal range.

**Conversion rate:** 1% to 3% is normal for a new store. Below 1% consistently means your product page or offer needs attention. Above 3% is strong and suggests your traffic is well-targeted and your listing is compelling.

**CPA:** This depends entirely on your product and price point. The benchmark that matters is yours: is your CPA below your margin per sale? If yes, you are acquiring customers profitably. If no, you need to improve the ads, the listing, or both.

**Email open rate:** 15% to 25% is healthy for e-commerce. Below 15% might mean your subject lines need work or your list includes people who are no longer engaged. Above 25% is excellent.

**Email list growth:** Any positive growth is good at this stage. Even 10 new subscribers a week adds up to over 500 a year, which is a meaningful audience for a small business.

Do not compare your numbers to large, established brands. Compare them to your own numbers last week. The trend matters more than the absolute number.

## Your weekly review ritual

You set a weekly review slot in Chapter 4. This is what you do during it.

**Step 1 (5 minutes): Collect your numbers.** Open your store dashboard, your ad platform, your email tool, and your spreadsheet. Write down this week's six numbers.

**Step 2 (5 minutes): Compare to last week.** Is each number up, down, or flat? Look for trends, not single-week spikes or dips. One bad week is noise. Two or three bad weeks in a row is a signal.

**Step 3 (5 minutes): Identify one thing that worked.** What drove the most traffic? Which product had the best conversion? What email had the highest open rate? Knowing what works tells you what to do more of.

**Step 4 (5 minutes): Identify one thing to change.** What underperformed? Where did you lose the most potential customers? Pick one specific thing to adjust this week. Not five things. One thing. Changing too many variables at once means you will never know which change made the difference.

**Step 5 (5 minutes): Plan next week.** Based on what you learned, what are you focusing on this week? Write it down. One or two priorities, not a long list.

Twenty to thirty minutes total. That is all it takes to run your business with real information instead of guesswork. The discipline of doing this every single week is what separates businesses that improve from businesses that drift.

## Update your Founder Rules

Your Founder Rules from Chapter 4 were written before you had real data. Now you have several weeks of real numbers. Revisit your rules and sharpen them:

Your kill criteria can now include specific numbers: "I will stop if conversion stays below 1% after two listing revisions and 500 visitors."

Your continue criteria can reference trends: "I will continue if CPA is trending downward week on week, even if it has not hit my target yet."

Your escalation criteria can be grounded in evidence: "I will invest more when CPA is consistently below £X and I have maintained a conversion rate above Y% for three consecutive weeks."

These updated rules are not just numbers on a page. They are the decision-making system that tells you exactly what to do when results come in. No more agonising over whether to keep going or try something new. The rules tell you.

## You are now managing a real business

Take a moment to recognise what is happening. You are not planning a business. You are not thinking about a business. You are running one, with real customers, real revenue, and real data that you review and act on every week.

Most people who set out to sell online never reach this point. You are here because you did the work: researched products, ran the numbers, tested on a marketplace, built an offer, launched a store, drove traffic, and started measuring.

The next chapter teaches you what to do when the numbers tell you something needs to change. Whether that means small adjustments or a bigger shift, you will have a clear framework for deciding.

# Chapter 15 — Iterate and Pivot

**Estimated reading time:** 10 minutes
**Worksheet:** Iteration Decision worksheet
**Canvas connection:** Trajectory tracking activates. If learner pivots, canvas archives current product data and opens fresh track. Pivot visible as part of journey, not a reset.

## Something is not working. That is normal.

If every part of your business was performing perfectly, you would not need this chapter. But no business runs perfectly, especially not a new one. Some ads will underperform. Some listings will not convert. Some products will sell slower than expected.

The good news is that you now have something most struggling sellers do not: data. You know your numbers. You know your trends. And you have decision rules that tell you when to adjust and when to change direction.

This chapter teaches you how to use all of that to make smart, evidence-based decisions about what to do next.

## Diagnose before you decide

The most common mistake when something is not working is to change everything at once or to give up entirely. Neither is usually the right move.

Before you decide what to do, figure out where the problem actually is. Your business is a chain: traffic arrives, visitors see your product page, some of them buy, and some of those come back. A problem at any link in that chain produces different symptoms and requires different solutions.

**Low traffic, everything else is fine.** People who find your store are buying, but not enough people are finding your store. The problem is your marketing channels (Chapters 11 and 12), not your product or listing. Solution: try new communities, adjust your ad targeting, increase your posting frequency, or test a new ad creative.

**Good traffic, low conversion rate.** People are finding your store but not buying. The problem is your product page, your price, or your offer. Solution: revisit your listing (Chapter 9), test a different price point (Chapter 8), improve your photos, or address objections you might have missed in your description.

**Good traffic, good conversion, but CPA too high.** People are buying, but it costs too much to get each customer. The problem is your advertising efficiency. Solution: refine your audience targeting, test new ad creative, or try a different ad platform. Sometimes a small change in the audience definition makes a significant difference to CPA.

**Good traffic, good conversion, but margin too thin.** You are selling but not keeping enough money per sale. The problem is your cost structure. Solution: negotiate better terms with your supplier, reduce shipping costs (different carrier, lighter packaging), raise your price slightly, or reduce your returns rate by improving product descriptions and photos so buyers know exactly what to expect.

**Some of everything but nothing stands out.** Mixed results with no clear pattern. This usually means you need more time or more data. Check your minimum experiment duration from Chapter 4. If you have not hit it, keep going before making changes.

[IMAGE: A simple diagnostic flowchart. Starting point: "Something is not working." First branch: "Are enough people visiting your store?" If no → "Marketing problem — Chapters 11-12." If yes → "Are visitors buying?" If no → "Listing or offer problem — Chapters 8-9." If yes → "Is CPA sustainable?" If no → "Ad efficiency problem — Chapter 12." If yes → "Is margin healthy?" If no → "Cost structure problem — Chapter 5." If yes → "Things are actually working — keep going." The flowchart should feel like a helpful troubleshooting guide, clean and easy to follow.]

## Iterate: small changes, one at a time

Once you have diagnosed the problem, make one change at a time and measure the result.

This is important. If you change your ad image, rewrite your description, and lower your price all in the same week, and sales go up, which change caused the improvement? You have no idea. You have fixed the problem but learned nothing, which means you cannot repeat the success or build on it.

**Test one variable per cycle.** If the diagnosis points to your ad creative, change the image or headline but keep the audience and budget the same. Run it for at least a week. Compare the numbers. Did CTR improve? Did CPA change?

If the diagnosis points to your listing, rewrite the opening paragraph of your description but keep the photos, price, and title the same. Observe the conversion rate over the next week.

Each test cycle gives you a clear data point: this specific change produced this specific result. Over time, these data points compound into a deep understanding of what works for your product and your customer.

**Keep a record of what you tested.** In your weekly review, note what you changed, why you changed it, and what happened. This record becomes increasingly valuable as it grows. Three months of documented experiments is a goldmine of knowledge about your business that no competitor has.

## When to pivot (and what that actually means)

A pivot is not failure. It is a disciplined response to evidence that tells you the current approach has run its course.

You should consider a pivot when:

**You have exhausted reasonable iterations and the numbers still do not work.** You have tested multiple ad creatives, rewritten your listing, adjusted your price, and tried different audiences. After several cycles, the fundamental metrics (conversion rate, CPA) are not moving toward viability. The individual elements have been optimised but the overall equation does not add up.

**The demand you thought existed was not as strong as the signals suggested.** Your pre-store test in Chapter 6 showed promise, but sustained sales at the volumes you need are not materialising. Sometimes a marketplace test captures a small pocket of demand that does not scale.

**A different opportunity has emerged with stronger evidence.** While running your current product, you discovered a related product with better demand signals, stronger margins, or a less competitive market. Your customer research and marketing experiments sometimes surface opportunities you did not see during the original brainstorming phase.

## What pivoting looks like in practice

A pivot does not mean starting from scratch. It means changing one or more of the core elements of your business while keeping everything else you have built.

**Product pivot:** Switch to a different product while keeping the same customer. You have already identified where your customer gathers and what they care about. A new product for the same audience lets you reuse your marketing channels, your email list, and your community presence.

**Customer pivot:** Take the same product to a different audience. Maybe the product works but you have been targeting the wrong buyer. A seat cover you positioned for dog owners might sell better positioned for parents of young children, or for delivery drivers who need to protect their seats.

**Channel pivot:** Keep the product and customer but change how you reach them. If Instagram ads are not working, try Google Shopping. If social media is expensive, lean harder into SEO and communities.

**Offer pivot:** Same product, same customer, different packaging. Change the price, the bundle, the positioning, or the promise. Sometimes a product that does not sell at £25 as a standalone item sells well at £40 as a bundle with a complementary accessory.

The key principle: change one element at a time so you can measure the impact. A pivot is still an experiment, not a guess.

## The pivot loop

This course is designed for exactly this situation. If you pivot, you do not go back to Chapter 1. You go back to the chapter that matches the type of pivot:

- **New product:** Return to Chapter 3 (Brainstorm) and Chapter 5 (Know Your Numbers). You will move through them much faster this time because you now have real experience with the research process and the maths.
- **New customer:** Return to Chapter 7 (Pick Your Customer). Your product and economics stay the same. You are just finding a better-fitting audience.
- **New channel:** Stay in Chapter 11 (Free Traffic) or Chapter 12 (Paid Ads) and test a different approach.
- **New offer:** Return to Chapter 8 (Shape Your Offer) and rework the packaging, price, or positioning.

Every skill you have built transfers. Every data point you have collected informs the next round. You are not starting over. You are starting from a much stronger position than the first time.

[LOOP: If you have decided to pivot, take a moment to write down what you learned from the product or approach you are leaving behind. What worked? What did not? What surprised you? This is not busywork. It is the raw material that makes your next attempt better. The most successful sellers are not the ones who got it right the first time. They are the ones who learned the most from each round.]

## When not to pivot

A bad week is not a reason to pivot. A seasonal dip is not a reason to pivot. A single negative review is not a reason to pivot. Impatience is the most common cause of premature pivots.

Go back to your minimum experiment duration from Chapter 4. Have you given this approach enough time? Have you tested enough iterations to be confident the problem is fundamental rather than fixable?

The data-over-ego commitment works in both directions. It means being willing to walk away from an idea you love when the evidence says so. It also means being willing to stick with an idea you are uncertain about when the evidence is still developing.

Patience, guided by data, is one of the most valuable things a founder can practise.

The next chapter is for the moment when the evidence says something wonderful: it is working. Your numbers are hitting your escalation criteria. Customers are coming. Revenue is growing. And you need to decide what to do with that momentum.

## You have earned this chapter

Your product is selling. Your CPA is within target. Conversion is stable. Customers are coming back. Revenue is growing week on week. Your escalation criteria from Chapter 4 are being met.

This is the moment most founders dream about. It is also the moment where many of them make mistakes that undo everything they have built. Growing too fast, adding too many products, spreading too thin, increasing ad spend before the infrastructure can handle it.

This chapter teaches you how to grow without breaking what is working. The same discipline that got you here is the discipline that scales you further.

## Recognise what "working" actually looks like

Before you start planning for growth, make sure the signals are real and sustained. A single great week is not a trend. You are looking for:

**Consistent performance over at least four weeks.** Conversion rate is stable. CPA is within your target range. Revenue is growing or holding steady. A single spike caused by a viral social media post or a seasonal bump is encouraging but it is not a foundation for growth decisions.

**Positive unit economics confirmed by real data.** Your actual margin per sale (from your records, not your projection from Chapter 5) is healthy. Returns are within your expected range. Costs have not crept up. The maths works in practice, not just on paper.

**Systems that are functioning.** Orders are shipped on time. Customer questions are answered promptly. Your weekly review is happening consistently. Your email sequences are running. If any of these are falling behind, growth will make the problem worse, not better.

If all three are true, you are ready to grow. If one or more is shaky, strengthen it first. Growth amplifies everything, including problems.

## Scale versus grow: know the difference

**Growing** means doing more of the same. More ad spend to get more customers. More products to serve more needs. More hours invested to handle more volume. Your revenue goes up, but so does your workload proportionally.

**Scaling** means increasing revenue without proportionally increasing your time or cost. Automating your email sequences. Using ad platform algorithms to optimise spending. Improving your conversion rate so more visitors buy without additional ad spend. Negotiating better supplier terms as your volumes increase.

Both have their place. Early on, you will mostly grow (more of what works). Over time, you want to shift toward scaling (better systems, more efficiency).

The important thing is to be intentional about which one you are doing, because they require different approaches and have different demands on your time and budget.

## Increase ad spend gradually

If your paid ads are profitable, the natural instinct is to double the budget and double the sales. It rarely works that cleanly.

Ad platforms work on audience segments. Your current budget might be reaching the most responsive segment of your target audience. When you increase the budget, the platform starts showing your ad to slightly less responsive people within your targeting. This often means CPA rises as you spend more.

**The safe approach:** Increase your ad budget by 20% to 30% at a time. Run for at least a week at the new level. Check whether your CPA remains within your acceptable range. If it does, increase again. If CPA rises above your target, pull back to the previous level.

This sounds slow. It is. It is also how you scale ad spend without losing money. A gradual, data-guided increase protects the profitability you worked hard to build.

## Add products or go deeper?

When your first product is selling well, you will start thinking about adding more. That might be the right move, but ask yourself these questions first:

**Is there more to gain from the current product?** Could you improve the listing, test new ad creatives, try new marketing channels, or raise the price? Sometimes the biggest growth opportunity is going deeper on what already works, not adding something new.

**Does a second product serve the same customer?** The strongest product additions are ones your existing customer would naturally buy alongside your first product. A dog seat cover seller adding a car boot liner for dog owners makes sense. The same seller adding a yoga mat does not. Your email list, your ad targeting, and your brand all work harder when products are related.

**Can you handle the operational complexity?** Each new product means new supplier relationships (or more orders from existing ones), new listings to write and maintain, new stock to manage (if physical), new photos, and new variables to track. If your current systems are straining to handle one product, adding a second will make things harder before they get better.

If you do add a product, apply the same process you used for your first: validate demand (Chapter 3), run the numbers (Chapter 5), test on a marketplace if possible (Chapter 6), and only commit resources once you have evidence.

## A second store: when it makes sense

At some point, you might consider opening a second store for a completely different product category or market. Here is the honest assessment.

**When it makes sense:** Your first store is operationally stable. It could run for a week without your daily involvement because your systems (email automation, ad campaigns, fulfilment process) are solid. You have identified a genuinely different market with strong demand evidence. Your Founder Rules can accommodate the additional time and budget.

**When it does not make sense:** Your first store still needs significant attention. You are spending most of your available hours managing operations rather than growing. You are drawn to a second store because the first one is boring (this is a feeling, not a data-driven reason to diversify).

**The honest reality:** Running two stores is not twice as hard operationally if your processes are solid. Order fulfilment, email sequences, and ad management follow the same patterns regardless of the product. But it is roughly twice the mental load. Your attention is your scarcest resource, and splitting it means each store gets less of it.

If you decide to go ahead, treat the second store like a new Chapter 2 to Chapter 10 journey. Apply everything you have learned, but do not assume what works for one store will work for another. Different products, different customers, different dynamics.

The message: running two stores is about clear systems and honest resource allocation.]

## Update your Founder Rules for the next phase

Your business has changed. Your rules should change with it.

**Time budget:** Are you spending more hours now? Are they productive hours or are you filling time with low-value tasks? Growth sometimes means spending the same hours on higher-impact activities rather than simply spending more hours.

**Money cap:** If your business is generating revenue, you might choose to reinvest some of that into growth. Your money cap can shift from "what I can afford to spend from personal savings" to "what the business earns minus what I keep as profit." This is a meaningful transition because the business is now funding its own growth.

**Decision thresholds:** Your kill, continue, and escalation criteria should be recalibrated for a more mature business. What was "escalation" for a brand new store (conversion above 3%) might be "continue" for an established one. Raise the bar as your experience and data grow.

**Red-line rules:** Review your non-negotiables. Some may need updating. "No spending above £300/month" might become "No spending above £800/month unless CPA remains below £X." Others should stay exactly as they are. "No skipping weekly review" applies regardless of how successful the business becomes.

## Growth is a continuation, not a destination

There is no finish line where your business is "done." There is an ongoing cycle of measuring, adjusting, and improving. The habits you built in Chapter 4 (rules), Chapter 14 (weekly review), and Chapter 15 (iteration) are not training wheels you remove once the business is running. They are the operating system that keeps it running well.

The final chapter gives you a permanent home for all of this: your operating dashboard. A place to enter your weekly numbers, track your trends, and keep your entire business picture in one view. Everything you have built across 16 chapters comes together in one place.

You are almost there.

## The weekly review is your most important habit

You have been doing weekly reviews since Chapter 4. By now it should feel like a natural part of your routine. This chapter formalises it into a permanent practice and gives it a permanent home.

The weekly review is not optional maintenance. It is the single activity that keeps everything else working. Without it, problems go unnoticed until they become expensive. Opportunities get missed because nobody was looking. And the discipline you built over the course slowly erodes as decisions drift back toward guesswork.

With it, you catch issues early, double down on what is working, and make decisions based on evidence every single week.

Twenty to thirty minutes. That is the cost. The return is a business you understand and control.

## What to review and when

Not every number needs checking every week. Different metrics operate on different timescales. Here is a simple cadence that covers everything without consuming your entire schedule.

### Weekly (during your review slot)

These are your vital signs. Check them every week:

**Revenue and orders.** How much did you make? How many orders? Compare to last week and to your 4-week average. A single slow week is normal. Three slow weeks in a row needs investigation.

**Traffic.** How many visitors? From which sources? Is any channel growing or shrinking? If you are running ads, how is the traffic from paid channels compared to free channels?

**Conversion rate.** Are visitors buying at the same rate as before? A drop in conversion with stable traffic usually points to a listing, pricing, or trust issue.

**CPA.** If you are running ads, what did each customer cost this week? Is it within your target range? Trending up or down?

**Email list growth.** How many new subscribers? Are your signup incentives still working?

**One thing that worked. One thing to change.** Write both down. Over time, this creates a decision log that is incredibly valuable for spotting patterns.

### Monthly (set a calendar reminder)

These need a slightly longer look:

**Actual margin per sale.** Recalculate using real numbers: actual product costs, actual shipping costs, actual fees, actual returns. Compare to your projection from Chapter 5. If they are diverging, find out why.

**Channel performance.** Which traffic sources are delivering the best return? Is one channel costing more than it is worth? Should you shift budget or effort from one to another?

**Email performance.** Open rates, click rates, and revenue from email campaigns. Are your automated sequences still performing? Do they need refreshing?

**Customer feedback.** What are customers saying in reviews, messages, and emails? Any recurring complaints or requests? Patterns in feedback are product development opportunities.

### Quarterly (every three months)

This is the big picture review:

**Revisit your Founder Rules.** Is your time budget still realistic? Has your financial situation changed? Do your decision thresholds need recalibrating? Your rules should evolve as your business matures.

**Assess your positioning.** Has the competitive landscape changed? Are new sellers entering your market? Has your customer's problem shifted? Your offer positioning from Chapter 8 should still be accurate and relevant.

**Growth direction check.** Are you where you expected to be three months ago? What went better than expected? What took longer? Based on the trajectory, what should the next three months focus on?

**Product range decision.** Is it time to add a product, remove one, or adjust your range? Use the same evidence-based approach from Chapters 3 and 5.

## Entering your metrics

Your operating dashboard is where you log your weekly numbers. Over time, this becomes the most valuable record your business has. It is the history of what happened, what you tried, and what worked.

Each week, you enter:

- Revenue
- Number of orders
- Traffic (visitors)
- Ad spend (if running ads)
- New email subscribers
- Refunds or returns
- What worked this week
- What to change next week
- Any notes worth recording

The dashboard tracks these over time, so you can see trends at a glance. A conversion rate that looks fine in isolation might reveal a slow decline when you see it plotted across twelve weeks. A CPA that seems high this week might actually be part of a downward trend that shows your optimisation is working.

Consistency matters more than precision. A rough weekly entry is infinitely more useful than a perfect monthly entry. Get the numbers in every week, even if they are approximate. The habit is what matters.

## How to read your dashboard

Numbers without context are just numbers. Here is how to interpret what your dashboard shows you:

**Stable metrics are good news.** If your conversion rate, CPA, and margin have held steady for four or more weeks, your business is running reliably. Stability gives you a foundation to test changes from, because you have a clear baseline to compare against.

**Gradual trends matter more than single-week changes.** A 20% drop in traffic in one week could be a platform glitch, a seasonal dip, or a temporary algorithm change. A 5% decline in traffic every week for six consecutive weeks is a pattern that needs attention. Train your eye to see the trend line, not the individual data points.

**Connected metrics tell a story.** If your traffic is up but revenue is flat, your conversion rate has dropped. If your CPA is rising but conversion is stable, your ads are getting more expensive (possibly due to increased competition or ad fatigue). If your email list is growing but email revenue is not, your emails may need refreshing. The dashboard shows you these relationships when you track everything in one place.

**Know when to be concerned and when to be patient.** Two consecutive weeks of declining conversion is worth investigating. Two consecutive weeks of slightly lower traffic during a known quiet period (school holidays, summer, post-Christmas) is probably seasonal. Context matters. This is why keeping notes in your weekly review is valuable: six months from now, you can look back and see that the slow period was normal.

## When the dashboard shows trouble

Your dashboard will occasionally show you things you do not want to see. Revenue dropping. CPA rising. Conversion falling. When this happens, resist the urge to panic or make sweeping changes.

Go back to the diagnostic framework from Chapter 15. Where in the chain is the problem? Traffic, conversion, CPA, or margin? Diagnose, then adjust one thing, then measure.

Your kill criteria from Chapter 4 (updated with real numbers in Chapter 14) tell you when the situation is serious enough to warrant a bigger decision. If the numbers are approaching your kill thresholds, it is time for the honest conversation: iterate harder, pivot, or stop and redirect your resources to something with better evidence.

The dashboard is not there to make you feel good. It is there to tell you the truth. And the truth, even when it is uncomfortable, is always more useful than not knowing.
